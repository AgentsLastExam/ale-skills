# Chemistry: mechanism and properties

This file adds content specific to mechanism and properties to `references/disciplines/chemistry.md`, whose provisions still apply.

Scope: in physical organic and physical inorganic chemistry, distinguishing reaction mechanisms (concerted or stepwise, radical or polar), the origins of stereoselectivity and regioselectivity, structure–property relationships such as substituent effects and linear free-energy relationships, and the mechanisms of excited-state and photoredox reactions. For catalytic mechanisms on surfaces and electrodes and the turnover frequency of catalytic cycles see `catalysis.md`; for questions whose purpose is a new reaction or the prediction of conditions and yields see `synthesis.md`; for accurate rate constants, spectral assignment and ultrafast dynamics see `structure-dynamics.md`; for the development and benchmarking of electronic-structure methods themselves see `theory-computation.md`; for enzyme mechanisms see `life-processes.md`.

## Screening criteria

- Build with priority: the question comes down to free-energy differences within a set of competing transition states, and a measured selectivity exists to compare against. An example is the origin of enantioselectivity in asymmetric catalysis: transition-state calculations give the selectivity directly, and it can be compared with the free-energy difference converted from the experimental ee; the proline-catalyzed aldol reaction is of this kind.
- Build with priority: a choice among several candidate mechanisms that make different predictions for the same observable. An example is concerted versus stepwise, or radical versus polar: potential-energy-surface scans, intrinsic reaction coordinates and computed isotope effects are standard practice, and experimental results such as natural-abundance ¹³C kinetic isotope effects, Hammett correlations and kinetic orders exist to compare against.
- Defer: the mechanisms of photoredox and excited-state reactions. Excited-state and open-shell calculations are expensive and the results are sensitive to the choice of method. Discuss with the expert a formulation that fixes the method and active space in advance and asks only for sub-problems that can be compared with measured quantities, for example an excited-state redox potential, whether a quenching step is thermodynamically feasible, or whether the quantum yield is far above 1 (the criterion for a chain mechanism).
- Defer: questions in which the selectivity is determined by explicit solvent, ion pairs or aggregates and the model cannot be fixed in advance, for example selectivity determined by the aggregation state of an organolithium reagent; and systems whose experimental ee comes from a single source and is sensitive to water content and temperature. The former can be recast as a comparison within an aggregate model specified by the expert, the latter by replacing the absolute value with the ordering of selectivity across a set of substrates.

## Matters to confirm in conversation

- The experimental basis of the selectivity or mechanistic conclusion to be explained: the conditions under which the product ratio (ee, dr, regioselectivity) was measured, namely temperature, solvent, concentration, catalyst loading and additives, and whether it has been repeated.
- Whether the selectivity is under kinetic or thermodynamic control; which step is stereodetermining, whether it is reversible, and whether it is the same as the rate-determining step.
- The list of candidate mechanisms, and the mechanisms already ruled out with their experimental grounds (isotope labeling, nonlinear effects, crossover experiments, kinetic orders).
- The model system: whether substituents are truncated; the protonation state, tautomer and aggregation state of the catalyst; whether explicit solvent molecules or additives enter the transition state.
- The level of theory: the functional, dispersion correction, basis set and solvent model for geometry optimization and for single-point energies, the entropy treatment of low-frequency vibrations and the standard state, and whether the field accepts this setting.
- The method and extent of the conformer search; whether the ensemble of transition-state conformers is combined by Boltzmann weights or only the lowest one is taken.
- The form of the conclusion: the configuration of the major product, the value of a free-energy difference, the ordering of selectivity across a set of substrates, or a choice between mechanisms; what the tolerance rests on (method error, repeatability of the experimental ee).
- Whether auxiliary observables such as isotope effects and Hammett ρ values are among the deliverables.

## Checking templates

The templates below add to the general templates in `references/disciplines/chemistry.md`.

| Template | Criterion | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Recomputation on the transition-state structures | At the level fixed in the task statement, the agent judge runs single-point and frequency calculations on each submitted transition state: there is exactly one imaginary frequency, its mode involves the atoms forming and breaking bonds, and the intrinsic reaction coordinate connects the correct reactants and products at its two ends; the free energies agree with the values the submitter reports. For a structure that fails, the corresponding values score nothing | Pure computation, must pass |
| Reconversion of the selectivity | From the free energies of all submitted transition-state conformers, the agent judge recomputes the product ratio and ΔΔG‡ with Boltzmann weights: the configuration of the major product agrees with experiment, and ΔΔG‡ agrees within tolerance with the value converted from the experimental ee | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 1–2 |
| Conformer coverage | The lowest submitted transition state is, within tolerance, no higher than the lowest one of the reference computation; every conformer with significant weight in the reference ensemble has been found | Pure computation, counts for part of the score |
| Isotope effects | From the submitted frequencies of reactant and transition state, the agent judge recomputes the kinetic isotope effects with the Bigeleisen–Mayer equation and compares them with the measured values, to discriminate between candidate mechanisms | Uses existing data |
| Choice between mechanisms | The highest barriers of the candidate mechanisms are compared on the same energy reference; a mechanism that is ruled out must come with the structures and energies of its transition states or intermediates, not with a verbal argument alone | Pure computation, scientific discovery level 2–3 |
| Linear free-energy relationship | Barriers or equilibrium constants are recomputed for a set of substituents; the slope of the regression against substituent constants (the ρ value) agrees with experiment in sign and magnitude | Uses existing data |
| Prediction for held-out substrates | Checked on substrates not used in choosing the level of theory: the ordering of selectivity or the configuration of the major product agrees with experiment | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 2–3 |

## Sources of existing data

- Selectivity and mechanistic experiments: ee, dr, isotope effects and kinetic orders in the original papers and their supporting information. Values for the same reaction can differ between groups and between batches; check the measurement conditions before using them.
- Reactivity and thermodynamic parameters: Mayr's database of reactivity parameters (nucleophilicity and electrophilicity parameters); iBonD (pKa values and bond dissociation energies, for non-profit academic use only); compilations of Hammett substituent constants.
- Computed benchmarks: BH9 (barrier heights and reaction energies of 449 organic and biochemical reactions), the barrier-height subsets of GMTKN55, QUESTDB (theoretical best estimates of vertical excitation energies). These reference values are computed, so they count as pure computation, not existing data.

## Preventing answer leakage

- The mechanistic conclusions and major-product configurations of classic reactions are in textbooks and reviews, so scoring on the conclusion alone can be passed by searching. Make the submitted transition-state structures the object of scoring: the agent judge recomputes them, and values score only when the energies and the connectivity agree.
- The supporting information of published calculations often includes transition-state coordinates. The task statement uses a level of theory different from the literature, or substrates the literature has not computed, and the submitted structures are checked to be stationary points at that level.
- The experimental ee is public, so "the predicted ee is close to the experimental value" is not scored on its own; what scores is the ΔΔG‡ recomputed from the submitted structures and the ordering of selectivity for held-out substrates.
- The task statement contains no names of named reactions or named transition-state models, no paper titles and no DOIs; substrates and catalysts are given as structures.

## Tools and compute

- Conformer and transition-state search: xtb and CREST (conformer ensembles), autodE (automated transition-state search and reaction profiles from reactants and products), Sella and geomeTRIC (transition-state optimization).
- High-level single-point corrections: DLPNO-CCSD(T) in ORCA (free for academic use, may not be redistributed); in a fully open-source environment, hybrid and double-hybrid functionals in Psi4 or PySCF.
- Thermochemistry and selectivity: GoodVibes (quasi-harmonic corrections, Boltzmann weights and selectivity) and AQME (workflow automation), both under the MIT license.
- Isotope effects: Kinisot and PyQuiver (computed from harmonic frequencies with the Bigeleisen–Mayer equation, with tunneling corrections).
- Compute: for an organocatalytic system of thirty to forty atoms, solving one task requires optimizing tens to more than a hundred transition-state conformers and computing frequencies for each, on the order of one to several days on a workstation or a small cluster, with the conformer search and transition-state optimization taking most of the time; the review recomputes only single points and frequencies on the submitted structures and is far smaller than solving. These are rough estimates; the measurement in step 2 prevails.

## Common pitfalls

- Only one transition state computed per stereoisomer, with no conformer search. Recalculations ten years after the Houk–List model was proposed showed that a wider conformer search finds transition states of lower energy, and that the earlier quantitative agreement with experiment may have been coincidental.
- A tolerance set on the difference in ee. The relation between ee and ΔΔG‡ is not linear: at 298 K a ΔΔG‡ of 1.0 kcal/mol corresponds to 69% ee, 2.0 kcal/mol to 93% ee and 3.0 kcal/mol to 99% ee. The tolerance belongs on the free energy.
- A tolerance set tighter than the error of the method itself. For free-energy differences between competing transition states the computational error is on the order of a few tenths to 1 kcal/mol, and accuracy better than 1 kcal/mol is not routinely attainable.
- Low-frequency vibrations treated as harmonic oscillators, with an error in the entropy term large enough to change the predicted selectivity; the standard state and the quasi-harmonic correction must be the same for all species.
- The stereodetermining step assumed to be the rate-determining step, or its reversibility ignored. When the product racemizes or reverts under the reaction conditions, the measured ee does not reflect the energy difference between transition states.
- Transition states weighted by the ground-state populations of intermediates that interconvert faster than they react (for example the syn and anti enamines), in violation of the Curtin–Hammett principle.
- Dispersion corrections and basis-set superposition error have a marked effect on the relative energies of crowded transition states, and the predicted selectivity can reverse with another functional; the conclusion must state its dependence on the level of theory.
- The experimental ee is sensitive to water content, temperature and catalyst loading and differs between sources, yet a single value is used for comparison with no range given.
- A choice between mechanisms made on barriers alone, without testing what each mechanism predicts for other observables such as isotope effects and kinetic orders; or a mechanism ruled out without being computed.

## Example: the origin of enantioselectivity in the proline-catalyzed aldol reaction

In 2000 List, Lerner and Barbas reported that L-proline (30 mol%) catalyzes the direct asymmetric aldol reaction of acetone with aldehydes in a 4 : 1 mixture of DMSO and acetone at room temperature: 4-nitrobenzaldehyde gives 76% ee (68% yield) and isobutyraldehyde gives 96% ee (97% yield). In the accepted Houk–List model the reaction proceeds through an enamine intermediate, the carboxylic acid transfers its proton to the carbonyl oxygen of the aldehyde as the carbon–carbon bond forms, and the transition state in which the anti enamine attacks the re face of the aldehyde is the lowest in energy. The task asks the solving agent to obtain the major enantiomer and the origin of the selectivity independently by computation alone.

The following is illustrated for the Explanation class; the class is assigned by the expert. Scientific discovery level 2, physical-world observation level 1.

- **Task statement and deliverables.** The task statement gives the reactants, catalyst, solvent and temperature and the experimental ee for the two aldehydes, and fixes the level of theory (functional and dispersion correction, basis set, solvent model, treatment of low-frequency vibrations); it provides no names of mechanisms or models, no transition-state structures and no computed values from the literature. The deliverables are structure files of each transition state and the relevant intermediates, a `results.json` collecting the free energies and Boltzmann weights, and a short report. The format of the structure files and of `results.json` is the unified output format.
- **Sub-questions.** Divided by scientific content, for illustration only:
  1. Transition states of the carbon–carbon bond-forming step: with 4-nitrobenzaldehyde as the substrate, find the full conformer ensemble of each of the four types of transition state (syn or anti enamine, attack on the re or si face of the aldehyde).
  2. Origin of the selectivity: obtain the major enantiomer and ΔΔG‡ from the ensemble free energies, and state the respective roles of the proton transfer from the carboxylic acid and of the orientation of the aldehyde substituent.
  3. Choice between mechanisms and substrate comparison: compare on the same energy reference with at least one mechanism that has been ruled out (for example a transition state involving two proline molecules); repeat the calculation for isobutyraldehyde and give the order of ΔΔG‡ for the two aldehydes.
- **Given and deliverable.** The forms shown are for illustration; the expert decides which each sub-question needs. In every sub-question the given is the phenomenon (the experimental ee and the ΔΔG‡ converted from it, at 298 K 1.18 kcal/mol for 4-nitrobenzaldehyde and 2.31 kcal/mol for isobutyraldehyde). The deliverable is the mechanism, as computation (transition-state structures and conformer ensembles) and as mathematics (the relation that leads from free energies to the product ratio through the Curtin–Hammett principle and transition-state theory). The causal claim of sub-question 2, "the proton transfer from the carboxylic acid determines the face of attack", is established by ablation: the carboxylic acid is replaced by a group that cannot donate a proton (for example the methyl ester), the calculation is repeated, and the energy difference between the two faces of attack is examined to see whether it disappears.
- **Checks.** The agent judge recomputes the submitted transition states at the fixed level, checks the imaginary frequencies, the connectivity and the free energies the submitter reports, then reconverts the selectivity from all conformers: the major enantiomer agrees with experiment and the type of the lowest transition state agrees with the reference computation; ΔΔG‡ deviates by no more than 0.5 kcal/mol from the reference computation and by no more than 1 kcal/mol from the value converted from experiment. The tolerances are provisional; the former is set from the spread caused by different conformer searches and numerical settings in trial calculations, the latter from the method error and the repeatability of the experimental ee.
- **Tiers and scoring.** The four types of transition state for 4-nitrobenzaldehyde and the major enantiomer form the first tier, the ablation and the choice between mechanisms the second, and the comparison with isobutyraldehyde the third; completing only the first tier earns the corresponding score. The weights are set by the expert.
- **Calibration cases.** The empty submission (score 0); the conclusion of the literature model restated without transition-state structures, or structures whose recomputation disagrees with the reported values (a shortcut; the corresponding values score 0); a submission that argues for the two-proline mechanism (an explanation that has been ruled out); a submission with one conformer per type of transition state, the major enantiomer correct but ΔΔG‡ outside the tolerance (partially correct); the reference computation itself (a known result).
- **Other tasks of the same structure.** Change the catalyst (proline derivatives, chiral phosphoric acids) or the reaction (the Mannich reaction, endo versus exo selectivity in the Diels–Alder reaction); the workflow and the checks stay the same.
