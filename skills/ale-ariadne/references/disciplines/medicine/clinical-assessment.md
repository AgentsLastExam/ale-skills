# Medicine: clinical assessment and digital phenotyping

This file adds content specific to clinical assessment and digital phenotyping to `references/disciplines/medicine.md`, whose provisions still apply.

Scope: scales and psychometrics (item response theory and adaptive testing, instrument validity and cross-cultural equivalence), wearables and passive sensing (actigraphy, inference of sleep and activity, digital biomarkers and continuous monitoring, speech and keystroke-derived markers), mental health assessment and intervention (risk stratification and prediction of treatment outcome in psychiatry), function, rehabilitation and sports medicine (gait, motion capture and functional testing), and pain and symptom measurement (symptom clusters and trajectory modeling). For bedside and critical-care monitoring with signals such as ECG and EEG see `physiological-signals.md`; for the design and analysis of a scale or digital measure used as a trial endpoint see `clinical-trials.md`; for the assay performance of markers in blood and other fluids see `laboratory-medicine.md`; for the acquisition and quantification of imaging markers see `medical-imaging.md`.

## Screening criteria

- Build with priority: questions in which the inference from a wearable can be compared epoch by epoch with a reference standard recorded at the same time. An example is sleep–wake classification from wrist acceleration and heart rate: in one public dataset 31 participants wore an Apple Watch while undergoing polysomnography, and the criteria are epoch-wise sensitivity, specificity and Cohen's κ, with Bland–Altman limits of agreement for total sleep time.
- Build with priority: activity recognition in free-living conditions, with annotations that can be held out by participant. An example is CAPTURE-24: 151 participants wore a wrist accelerometer for about 24 hours, annotated from a wearable camera and sleep diaries, and its benchmark is scored on 10-second windows.
- Build with priority: questions on the measurement properties of a scale for which public item-level response data exists. An example is testing differential item functioning of a depression, anxiety and stress scale between populations with item response theory: the Open-Source Psychometrics Project has released 39,775 item-level responses to that scale, and the reference analysis can be run in full.
- Defer: tasks that predict depression or another mental state from passive sensing data when the only usable data is controlled-access, for example evaluating generalization across years and populations (GLOBEM holds 705 person-years of data from 497 participants and requires credentialing and a signed data use agreement). Discuss with the expert a switch to the expert's own cohort that may be put into a task.
- Defer: tasks whose object of judgement is the individual prediction of a rare outcome, for example risk stratification for suicide death: in one systematic review most models had a global classification accuracy of at least 0.80, yet a positive predictive value for suicide death of no more than 0.01; the outcome can be observed only after years of follow-up, and the data is mostly controlled-access. The task can instead derive and recompute the positive predictive value, net benefit and number needed to screen at a given prevalence.

## Matters to confirm in conversation

- The construct measured and its reference standard: a scale total score, a diagnosis by structured interview, polysomnography, motion capture or a clinical rating; the inter-rater agreement and test–retest reliability of the reference standard itself. The measurement properties of a scale are usually assessed one by one following the COSMIN taxonomy.
- The version, language, scoring rule and cutoff of the scale, the validation population behind the cutoff, and the copyright and license of the scale.
- The device and recording conditions: device model, wearing position, sampling rate, whether the data is the raw signal or the output of a vendor algorithm, and whether firmware and algorithm versions are fixed; which layer of the V3 framework (verification, analytical validation, clinical validation) the task sits in.
- The unit of analysis and the unit of splitting: epoch, night, day or person; whether all recordings of one participant go to the same side of the split.
- The outcome definition in questions on predicting treatment outcome: the criteria for response and remission (the scale used, the threshold of reduction, the assessment time point), whether raters were blinded, and how dropouts are handled.
- The use of the marker: prognosis (whatever the treatment), prediction of response to one treatment, or choosing between treatments; the latter two need data from a placebo group or another treatment group.
- How the external cohort differs from the development cohort: center, device or scanner, population and acquisition protocol; whether multi-center data was harmonized, and whether the harmonization was fitted after the split on the development part only.
- How large a difference is meaningful: the minimal clinically important difference and the standard error of measurement, and how the tolerance relates to both.

## Checking templates

The templates below add to the general templates in `references/disciplines/medicine.md`.

| Template | Criterion | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Epoch-wise agreement with a simultaneous reference standard | With the reference annotations of the held-out participants, the agent judge recomputes sensitivity, specificity and Cohen's κ from the submitted epoch-wise labels, and computes the Bland–Altman bias and limits of agreement for summary quantities (total sleep time, step count) | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 1–2 |
| Review of the split by participant | The agent judge confirms in the split file that no participant appears on both sides, and reruns the submitted pipeline with participants held out; the results agree with the values the submitter reports | Must pass |
| Refitting the measurement model | With the model specification fixed in the task statement, the agent judge refits the item response model or factor model; item parameters, fit indices and the calls on differential item functioning agree with the reference analysis within tolerance | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 1–2 |
| Discrimination and calibration on an external cohort | For a predictor fixed in advance, AUROC with its confidence interval, calibration and the P value of a permutation test are recomputed on an independent cohort and compared with the internal cross-validation estimate, giving the size of the drop | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 2–3 |
| Ablation for incremental value | The model is retrained with the marker features removed and only clinical variables kept, then scored on the external cohort; the gain in AUROC due to the markers, with its confidence interval, must reach the amount the submitter declared | Uses existing data, counts for part of the score |
| Treatment specificity | The same predictor is applied to a placebo group or another treatment group, where discrimination should be near chance; or the interaction between the marker and treatment assignment is tested | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 3 |

## Sources of existing data

- Sleep and activity: on PhysioNet, the dataset with wrist acceleration, heart rate and polysomnography recorded together (31 people) and Sleep-EDF (197 whole-night polysomnography recordings), both under open licenses; CAPTURE-24; the accelerometer data of NHANES. Cohorts such as MESA on the National Sleep Research Resource need an application and a signed data use agreement; the accelerometer data of UK Biobank is controlled-access.
- Mental health and passive sensing: Depresjon (wrist actigraphy of 23 patients with depression and 32 controls, with MADRS scores; the only condition its page states is that publications cite the original study); GLOBEM (controlled-access); DAIC-WOZ (audio, transcripts and facial features of 189 clinical interviews, available only to academic and non-profit researchers who sign a request form).
- Psychiatric markers and treatment outcome: the data of trials such as EMBARC is held in the NIMH Data Archive, where individual-level data needs approval and cannot be redistributed; TDBRAIN (resting-state EEG of 1,274 psychiatric patients, with about 30% of the clinical and treatment outcome data kept blinded for prospective validation; download requires a login and a signed data use agreement and the data may not be passed on to others).
- Scales: the anonymous item-level response data of the Open-Source Psychometrics Project; the PROMIS item banks and their item parameters are published by HealthMeasures. Copyright and license differ between scales and between translations; check each one before putting it into a task.
- Gait and movement: the Gait in Parkinson's Disease database on PhysioNet (vertical ground reaction force under the feet of 93 patients and 73 controls, with Hoehn and Yahr stage and UPDRS scores, under an open license).

## Preventing answer leakage

- When several recordings, nights or visits of one participant are split at random by recording, the model recognizes the person from individual traits and not the state, and accuracy is greatly overestimated. Always split by participant; for multi-center data also hold out by center.
- Feature selection, normalization, harmonization across centers and hyperparameter tuning all belong inside the inner loop of cross-validation. If any one of these steps is done on the full data, information from the held-out part has leaked.
- Labels of public datasets can be downloaded. Keep the reference annotations of held-out participants only in `tests/`; where possible use a part whose outcomes the data provider has not released, or the expert's own unpublished cohort.
- Published marker lists, cutoffs and AUROC values can be found by search. Make the submitted per-case predictions and the rerunnable pipeline the object of scoring; the task statement contains no trial name, registration number or dataset name.
- Demographic fields in scale response data, and timestamps and serial numbers in device data, may correlate with the outcome or be enough to identify a person. Check each one and remove it before putting the data into a task.

## Tools and compute

- Psychometrics: in R, mirt (item response theory), lavaan (factor models and measurement invariance) and psych; mirtCAT (simulation of adaptive tests).
- Accelerometry and sleep: GGIR, biobankAccelerometerAnalysis; YASA (automatic staging from polysomnography).
- Speech, EEG and imaging features: openSMILE (the open-source version is limited to private, research and educational use, and use in a commercial product needs a separate license), librosa; MNE-Python; FreeSurfer.
- Prediction and validation: scikit-learn (nested cross-validation, permutation tests), pROC in R; harmonization across centers commonly uses an implementation of ComBat (such as neuroCombat).
- Compute: scale analysis and accelerometer feature extraction run on CPUs in minutes to hours; training deep models on raw acceleration, speech or images needs GPUs; during review the recomputation of metrics takes minutes, and rerunning nested cross-validation and permutation tests takes tens of minutes to hours. These are rough estimates; the measurement in step 2 prevails.

## Common pitfalls

- A very high accuracy obtained by leave-one-out cross-validation on a small sample. With a small test set the estimate fluctuates widely: in a resampling study on structural MRI of 1,868 people, accuracy on the full sample was 61%, while subsamples of 20 people could give accuracies as high as 95%.
- Internal cross-validation performance taken as performance that generalizes. One model of antidepressant outcome had an internal cross-validated accuracy of 64.6% and 51.4%–59.7% in the three treatment groups of another trial; one model of antipsychotic outcome did no better than chance when applied to other trials.
- "Predicts response under one treatment" stated as "can be used to choose between treatments". The former is mixed with placebo response and natural course; the latter requires showing an interaction between the marker and treatment assignment.
- A continuous outcome dichotomized at a threshold such as a 50% reduction in the scale score and then predicted, which loses information and makes the result depend on the threshold and on baseline severity; regression to the mean also produces apparent response.
- Actigraphy detects wake less well than it detects sleep. Reporting only overall agreement or sensitivity hides a very low specificity; report each class separately and give κ.
- Total scores compared between populations of different language or culture without first testing measurement invariance. Differential item functioning can produce spurious group differences.
- Accuracy from studies with different reference standards compared directly. For the PHQ-9 at a cutoff of 10, sensitivity was 0.88 and specificity 0.85 against semi-structured interviews, and sensitivity was lower against fully structured interviews.
- Missingness in passive sensing data is not random. A device not worn or a phone switched off is often related to the state itself, and simple deletion or imputation introduces bias.
- In multi-center imaging or EEG data, center effects are confounded with the diagnostic mix, and the model learns the center and not the disease; a harmonization step that used data from the held-out part is a leak as well.

## Example: a biomarker predictor of antidepressant response and its external validation

The north-star problem is whether an objective biomarker panel can prospectively predict an individual's response to psychiatric treatment with an AUC above 0.80 in independent cohorts, and improve patient outcomes when used to guide the choice of treatment. It counts as solved by a multi-center, blinded prospective validation replicated in independent external cohorts, and by a randomized trial of biomarker-guided treatment against usual care; both need newly recruited patients and cannot be judged here. Existing studies show where the difficulty lies: the SMART-D trial used biobehavioral markers derived from the EMBARC study to guide assignment to sertraline or bupropion; the marker models had cross-validated AUCs of 0.66–0.86 in the training sample, and marker-positive patients had numerically higher response rates than patients negative on both markers (71.4% with both positive, 65.4% with one positive, 42.9% with both negative), yet the response rate of patients whose drug matched their markers (61.5%) was no higher than that of mismatched patients (66.7%, P = 0.72), and the trial was single-center with only 48 patients analyzed; in another study, which predicted response to ketamine in treatment-resistant depression from structural MRI, the AUC was 0.72 in the discovery cohort (99 people) and fell to 0.65 in the external cohorts (51 people). What can be built is a sub-problem within it: from existing data, develop a predictor of individual response whose discrimination must hold in an independent external cohort.

The following is illustrated for the Development class; the class is assigned by the expert. Scientific discovery level 3, physical-world observation level 1.

- **Task statement and deliverables.** Both cohorts must be de-identified data that the expert may put into a task; data held in controlled-access archives, such as EMBARC, cannot be included. The task statement gives the baseline features of the development cohort (clinical variables and marker features in separate columns) with response labels, the response criterion and the assessment time point, and gives only baseline features for the external cohort and the placebo group; it provides no trial name and no published model. The deliverables are per-case probabilities of response for the external cohort and the placebo group in `predictions.csv` (one column each for the full model and the model without markers), the external AUROC declared in advance with its interval in `results.json`, a rerunnable pipeline and a short report. The format of `predictions.csv` and `results.json` is the unified output format.
- **Sub-questions.** Divided by scientific content, for illustration only:
  1. Transferability of discrimination: the AUROC of the predictor on the external cohort, and its drop from the internal estimate.
  2. Incremental value of the markers: whether the markers raise discrimination on the external cohort beyond clinical variables such as baseline severity.
  3. Treatment specificity: whether what is predicted is response to this treatment, or general prognosis whatever the treatment.
- **Given and deliverable.** The forms shown are for illustration; the expert decides which each sub-question needs. In every sub-question the given is the part of the existing method the sub-question concerns, with the metric and the constraints to be maintained. The deliverable is the improvement and its contribution to the metric, as text (why the chosen markers relate to response, and why the measures against overfitting and for harmonization across centers do not break the constraint that the external cohort takes no part in development) and as computation (the improved pipeline and its metrics; the metrics with the marker features removed and only clinical variables kept, which is the ablation of the Development class; sub-question 3 uses discrimination in the placebo group as the control, and in the ketamine study above the AUC in the saline control group was 0.45). The agent judge reruns the pipeline and recomputes the metrics on the external cohort, and does not use the values the submitter reports.
- **Checks.** The agent judge reruns the pipeline, confirms that it reproduces the submitted probabilities and that the external cohort took no part in feature selection, harmonization or tuning; with the held-out labels it then computes the external AUROC with its bootstrap confidence interval and the P value of a permutation test, decides whether it exceeds the baseline with clinical variables only and whether it falls inside the interval the submitter declared, and computes the AUROC in the placebo group. For an external cohort of about 50 people and an AUROC of 0.65, the half-width of the 95% confidence interval estimated with the Hanley–McNeil formula is about 0.15; the tolerance is set from this, and the score follows the lower confidence limit, not the point estimate.
- **Calibration cases.** The empty submission (score 0); a pipeline that selects features on the two cohorts pooled and then performs "external validation" (a shortcut, identified by the trajectory check and by rerunning, score 0); a predictor with a very high internal cross-validated AUROC and an external AUROC near 0.5 (works only on the instances used in development, low score); a predictor whose external discrimination holds but does not drop when the markers are removed (partially correct); a logistic regression with clinical variables such as baseline severity only (the baseline).
- **What passing does not show.** The checks show only that, under this one split, the predictor discriminates better than the baseline in one independent cohort and that the markers add to it. This does not show that the performance can be repeated at more centers or under prospective, blinded conditions, does not show that the standard of an AUC above 0.80 has been met, and still less shows that assigning treatment by the predictor improves outcomes: in SMART-D, predictive ability and the benefit of guided prescribing came apart. The latter has to be decided by a randomized trial of biomarker-guided treatment against usual care, and is judged by the expert.
