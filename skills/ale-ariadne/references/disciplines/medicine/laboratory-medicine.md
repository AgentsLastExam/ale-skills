# Medicine: laboratory medicine and biomarkers

This file adds content specific to laboratory medicine and biomarkers to `references/disciplines/medicine.md`, whose provisions still apply.

Scope: clinical chemistry and hematology (routine panels and their derived indices, blood counts and flow cytometry), immunoassays and autoantibodies (serology and seroprevalence assays, autoantibody profiling), infectious diagnostics (molecular and point-of-care diagnostics, antimicrobial susceptibility testing), liquid biopsy and circulating markers (circulating tumor DNA and minimal residual disease), and assay quality and interpretation (reference intervals and standardization, analytical validation and lot-to-lot variation). For pathogen typing and prediction of resistance from genotype see `infectious-disease.md`; for the reading of markers on tissue sections see `pathology.md`; for the clinical interpretation of sequencing variants see `precision-medicine.md`; for the design of trials in which a marker guides treatment see `clinical-trials.md`.

## Screening criteria

- Build with priority: questions that re-estimate analytical performance from given raw measurement data under a written protocol. An example is estimating the limit of blank and the limit of detection from replicate measurements of blank and low-concentration samples, or a Passing–Bablok regression and Bland–Altman analysis of paired results from two creatinine methods: the EP documents of CLSI specify the experimental design and the algorithm, and the result is determined by the data.
- Build with priority: questions of result interpretation that public population data can answer. An example is establishing a reference interval for an analyte stratified by age and sex from the laboratory data of NHANES, or comparing how the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation and the earlier equation reclassify the stages of chronic kidney disease: the data are public and the statistic can be defined in advance.
- Build with priority: detection limits that can be derived and computed. An example is the sampling limit on the number of mutant molecules of circulating tumor DNA in a given blood volume and the ceiling it places on sensitivity, or correcting the apparent positive rate of a seroprevalence survey for the sensitivity and specificity of the assay: this is pure computation, and the inequalities in it can be written as Lean statements.
- Defer: questions whose criterion is prospective diagnostic accuracy in newly recruited participants, for example the sensitivity of a new multi-cancer early detection marker for stage I tumors. Discuss with the expert a re-test on an already public cohort with a threshold fixed in advance, or building only the sub-problems that can be checked on their own, such as the counting limit or the analytical performance.
- Defer: questions that depend on a particular instrument, reagent lot and sample matrix, for example variation between reagent lots, the commutability of reference materials and interference testing: the data are mostly not public, and the conclusion is hard to carry over to another laboratory. A repeatability measurement within one run can be finished within six hours and is physical-world observation level 2; the precision evaluation of CLSI EP05 runs over 20 days and is not built.

## Matters to confirm in conversation

- The measurand and the sample: analyte, matrix (serum, plasma, whole blood or capillary blood), anticoagulant, time from collection to separation and storage conditions; units and their conversion (between cfDNA mass and genome equivalents, at about 3.3 pg per haploid genome).
- The intended use: screening, diagnosis, monitoring or rule-out; the target population and its prevalence; who set the targets for sensitivity and specificity.
- The reference method or reference standard and its traceability: a reference measurement procedure, an international standard or a clinical diagnosis; the imprecision of the comparison method itself.
- The definitions of the performance measures and the protocol they follow: limit of blank, limit of detection and limit of quantitation, repeatability and within-laboratory imprecision, and the documents followed (the EP documents of CLSI, MIQE and dMIQE).
- Where the acceptance criteria come from: biological variation (the within-subject and between-subject variation listed in the EFLM Biological Variation Database), clinical outcomes, or the state of the art; whether regulatory or external quality assessment limits exist.
- The study design: consecutive enrollment or case-control; whether specimens were collected before diagnosis and whether measurement was blinded to the outcome; whether the threshold was fixed in advance.
- The minimum evidence for a positive call: the number of molecules, of positive reactions or of replicates; how specificity is allocated when several markers are combined into one call.
- The form of the conclusion and the tolerance: a value with its confidence interval (limit of detection, bias, reference limit), a pass or fail decision, or a bound and necessary conditions; what the tolerance rests on (variation between replicate measurements, the 90% confidence interval of a reference limit, biological variation).

## Checking templates

The templates below add to the general templates in `references/disciplines/medicine.md`.

| Template | Criterion | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Re-estimation of detection capability | From the replicate measurements of blank and low-concentration samples given in the task statement, the agent judge recomputes the limit of blank and the limit of detection under a fixed protocol (parametric: the limit of blank is the blank mean plus 1.645 standard deviations, the limit of detection is the limit of blank plus 1.645 standard deviations of the low-concentration sample; or the concentration at 95% detection probability from a probit regression) and compares them with the submitted values | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 1–2 |
| Method comparison | The slope and intercept of a Passing–Bablok or Deming regression of the paired results with their confidence intervals, and the Bland–Altman bias and limits of agreement; whether the bias at the medical decision levels exceeds an allowable limit given in advance | Uses existing data |
| Variance decomposition of precision | Repeatability and within-laboratory imprecision (variance components) re-estimated from replicate measurements in a nested design, compared with the claimed values and their upper confidence limits | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 1–2 |
| Reference interval | On held-out reference individuals the agent judge computes the fraction falling outside the submitted reference limits, and compares the limits with those from a fixed method (the nonparametric 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, with at least 120 reference individuals) and their 90% confidence intervals | Uses existing data |
| Diagnostic accuracy at a fixed threshold | With the threshold fixed in advance, sensitivity and specificity with exact confidence intervals are computed on a held-out cohort, or sensitivity at a given specificity; a threshold chosen on the same data is not accepted | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 2–3 |
| Derivation and numerical check of a counting limit | The parts of the conclusion that can be written as statements (an upper bound on the detection probability, the expected number of molecules needed for a target sensitivity) are checked with the Lean tool; the agent judge recomputes from the submitted parameters and cross-checks with a random-sampling simulation run | Pure computation, must pass |
| Conversion of prevalence and predictive values | The corrected prevalence, the positive and negative predictive values and their intervals are recomputed from the sensitivity and specificity of the assay and the apparent positive rate, and compared with the submitted values | Pure computation |

## Sources of existing data

- Population laboratory data: the laboratory data of NHANES are public; measurement methods and instruments changed between cycles, so check the conversion relations given in its documentation before use; hospital laboratory results mostly sit in controlled-access databases such as MIMIC-IV.
- Performance specifications: the EFLM Biological Variation Database (estimates of biological variation, publicly searchable); the breakpoint tables of EUCAST are free to obtain; CLSI documents must be purchased and any reproduction of them needs written permission from CLSI, so they cannot be distributed in a task package; the raw data of external quality assessment are mostly not public.
- Liquid biopsy: the multi-site SEQC2 evaluation of five ctDNA sequencing assays, carried out on reference samples derived from cell lines; cfDNA sequencing, fragmentomics and methylation data from patients are mostly under controlled access and may not be redistributed.
- Blood cells and flow cytometry: the public flow cytometry data in FlowRepository, including the datasets of the FlowCAP challenges; the site currently carries a suspension notice, so confirm the data can still be downloaded before relying on it.
- Literature values such as cfDNA concentration and tumor-derived fraction are, in derivation tasks, input parameters given in the task statement; a bound derived from them counts as pure computation, not existing data.

## Preventing answer leakage

- The conclusions and example numbers of derivation tasks are already published, for example the Poisson sampling limit and the 95% detection probability at an average of 3 copies. Use a parameter combination not found in the literature in the task statement, and make the submitted derivation and intermediate quantities that can be recomputed the object of scoring.
- Reference intervals, reclassification rates of equations and diagnostic accuracy on public data are mostly published. Ask for a stratum the paper did not report, or change the data cycle; the task statement contains no study names or registration numbers.
- One patient has several blood draws and several aliquots, and dilution series and replicate measurements come from one parent sample. Split by patient or by parent sample; replicates cannot sit on both sides.
- Choosing a threshold on the same data and then reporting accuracy amounts to letting the answer into the evaluation. The threshold is fixed on the development data, and the agent judge recomputes on the held-out cohort.
- Controlled-access patient data and exports from hospital laboratory systems cannot be put into a task package. Assay batch, collection date and sample identifier may point directly to case status, and when batch is confounded with case status a model can score from the batch alone; remove or shuffle them before providing the data.

## Tools and compute

- Method evaluation: in R, mcr (Passing–Bablok and Deming regression), VCA (variance components), refineR and referenceIntervals (reference intervals), pROC and epiR (diagnostic accuracy), drc (probit and dose-response fitting); Analyse-it, EP Evaluator and MedCalc need a commercial license.
- Liquid biopsy: fgbio and UMI-tools (deduplication and consensus sequences from molecular barcodes), bcftools, GATK Mutect2, ichorCNA (tumor-derived fraction from low-pass whole-genome sequencing); Bismark and MethylDackel for methylation analysis.
- Flow cytometry: flowCore and FlowSOM in Bioconductor; FlowJo needs a commercial license.
- Derivation and numerical checks: Lean 4 and Mathlib; SciPy and statsmodels (Poisson and binomial distributions, exact confidence intervals).
- Compute: method evaluation and derivation tasks take seconds to minutes on CPUs, and the recomputation during review is of the same scale; reanalysis of ctDNA sequencing data takes hours and needs tens to hundreds of GB of storage. These are rough estimates; the measurement in step 2 prevails.

## Common pitfalls

- The nominal limit of detection of an assay (stated as an allele fraction) used to argue that a sample can be detected, without counting the molecules in the sample. When the input genome equivalents are too few, the mutant molecule is not in the tube, whatever the sensitivity of the method.
- Analytical sensitivity (the limit of detection) confused with clinical sensitivity, or analytical specificity (freedom from interference and cross-reactivity) confused with clinical specificity.
- Accuracy estimated on a case-control sample of healthy controls and late-stage cases, then applied to a screening population. Sensitivity changes greatly with stage: one multi-cancer test reached 16.8% at stage I and 90.1% at stage IV, so it must be reported by stage.
- Only sensitivity and specificity reported at low prevalence. At a prevalence of 1%, a sensitivity of 50% and a specificity of 99.5%, the positive predictive value is only about one half.
- Agreement between two methods judged by a correlation coefficient or by ordinary least-squares regression. When both methods carry measurement error, use Deming or Passing–Bablok regression and give the bias from a Bland–Altman analysis.
- Limit of blank, limit of detection and limit of quantitation used interchangeably, or the blank mean plus a few standard deviations taken as the limit of detection with no low-concentration sample measured.
- A reference interval derived from too few reference individuals, without excluding diseased individuals, without stratifying by age and sex, or carried over directly between laboratories whose methods are not standardized.
- A tolerance set tighter than the imprecision of the measurement and the within-subject biological variation. The difference between two successive results of one person means something only when it exceeds the reference change value.
- Pre-analytical factors ignored. When whole blood stands too long, genomic DNA released by lysed white cells dilutes the tumor-derived fraction; hemolysis and the type of collection tube also change the result.

## Example: the molecule-counting limit of single-drop fingerstick ctDNA screening

The north-star problem is whether stage I multi-cancer early detection can be achieved from no more than 50 μL of fingerstick blood. It counts as solved when a prospective, blinded diagnostic study reaches a stage I sensitivity above 70% across the ten deadliest cancers at a specificity of at least 99.5%. That judgement needs newly recruited participants followed to a confirmed diagnosis, and cannot be made here. What can be built is the dry-lab sub-problem within it: the counting limit on the number of tumor-derived DNA molecules in the sampled volume. The arithmetic is as follows: the plasma of healthy people holds about 5–10 ng/mL of cfDNA, and a haploid genome weighs about 3.3 pg, so at the upper bound one milliliter of plasma holds at most about 10 ÷ 0.0033 ≈ 3,000 genome equivalents (about 2,270 at 7.5 ng/mL); one 20 μL drop of fingerstick blood yields about 10 μL of plasma, that is, at most about 30 genome equivalents; with the variant allele fraction of a stage I tumor taken as 10⁻⁴, the expected number of mutant molecules at a single locus is 30 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.003, and the probability that the sample holds at least one mutant molecule is 1 − exp(−0.003) ≈ 0.3%, which the single-locus sensitivity cannot exceed however sensitive the assay. Published data agree: in an analysis of 5,238 clinical plasma samples the median input, converted to 4 mL of plasma, was 5,531 genome equivalents, and at an allele fraction of 0.01% only about 10% of the samples had a 95% probability of holding at least one mutant molecule; a targeted methylation multi-cancer test on venous blood reached a stage I sensitivity of 16.8% at a specificity of 99.5%.

The following is illustrated for the Prediction class; the class is assigned by the expert. Scientific discovery level 3, physical-world observation level 0.

- **Task statement and deliverables.** The task statement gives the target (sensitivity above 70%, specificity at least 99.5%), a set of parameters with their ranges (blood volume, plasma yield, cfDNA concentration, mass per genome equivalent, tumor-derived fraction, molecule recovery, the minimum number of molecules k for a positive call) and Lean statements written in advance, with a parameter combination that differs from the published examples; it provides no conclusion and no reference values. The deliverables are the derivation, the Lean proofs, a `results.json` (the expected number of molecules, the sensitivity ceiling, and the volume and number of loci needed to reach the target, for each parameter combination), the computation code and a short report. The format of `results.json` is the unified output format.
- **Sub-questions.** Divided by scientific content, for illustration only:
  1. The counting limit for a single-locus mutation: give the sensitivity ceiling as a function of plasma volume, cfDNA concentration, tumor-derived fraction and k, and the plasma volume needed to reach 70%.
  2. Combining many loci: the number of loci needed when m mutually independent loci are combined into one call, and how low the false-positive rate per molecule must be to keep a specificity of 99.5%.
  3. Analytes not limited by genome copy number: for proteins, metabolites and the epigenetic state of white cells, compute the number of molecules or cells in one drop of blood, decide whether the counting limit still binds, and give the separation between cases and controls needed to reach the target.
- **Given and deliverable.** The forms shown are for illustration; the expert decides which each sub-question needs. In every sub-question the given is the target quantity as defined in the task statement; the true value is not provided to the solving agent. The deliverable is the prediction with its uncertainty, as text (the model and its conditions of validity: the number of mutant molecules follows a Poisson distribution when molecules enter the sample independently of one another and the fraction is very low; sub-question 3 uses an equal-variance binormal model) and as computation (the numerical results; a sensitivity analysis on each parameter, which is the ablation of the Prediction class: change the concentration, the fraction, the recovery and k one at a time and state which one decides the conclusion; the parameter range over which the conclusion holds). The agent judge compares the results with its own exact computation and a random-sampling simulation run.
- **Checks.** The Lean tool checks two statements: for any λ ≥ 0, the probability 1 − exp(−λ) that the sample holds at least one mutant molecule does not exceed λ; that probability is at least s (0 < s < 1) if and only if λ ≥ −ln(1 − s). The agent judge recomputes from the submitted parameters and confirms, with the parameters above as the example: λ = 0.003 and the ceiling is about 0.3%; at k = 1, reaching 70% requires λ ≥ 1.204, so a single locus requires about 12,000 genome equivalents (about 4 mL of plasma, equivalent to about 400 drops), or about 400 mutually independent loci combined; at k = 3 it requires λ ≥ 3.62; for a protein at 1 pg/mL with a molecular weight of 50 kDa, 10 μL of plasma holds about 1.2 × 10⁵ molecules, so the counting limit no longer binds, whereas under the equal-variance binormal model a specificity of 99.5% with a sensitivity of 70% requires the two group means to differ by about 3.1 standard deviations. All of these are exact results, and the tolerance allows only for rounding (1% relative); the tolerance of the simulation cross-check is 3 times its Monte Carlo standard error.
- **Calibration cases.** The empty submission (score 0); only the conclusion "infeasible", or copied literature values, with no derivation and no intermediate quantities that can be recomputed (a shortcut); feasibility argued from the nominal limit of detection of an assay, without counting molecules (a wrong model); a submission that completes only sub-question 1 (partially correct); the reference derivation itself (a known result).
- **What passing does not show.** The checks show only that, for the given parameters, the derivation and the numbers of the counting bound are correct, and that necessary conditions have been stated for analytes not limited by copy number. They do not show that any actual assay can reach the bound; they do not show the true distribution of the fraction in stage I tumors, and in the regression estimate of one study of early-stage lung cancer a primary tumor of 10 cm³ corresponded to a plasma allele fraction of about 0.1%, with smaller tumors lower; nor do they show that markers such as proteins or methylation in fact have the required separation between stage I cases and controls. Whether a fingerstick test can reach the target has to be decided by a prospective, blinded diagnostic study, and is judged by the expert.
