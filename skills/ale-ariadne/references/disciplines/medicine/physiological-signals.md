# Medicine: physiological signals and monitoring

This file adds content specific to physiological signals and monitoring to `references/disciplines/medicine.md`, whose provisions still apply.

Scope: cardiac electrophysiology and the ECG (arrhythmia detection and classification, deep learning on the ECG, cardiac electrophysiology simulation), EEG and neuromonitoring (seizure detection and prediction, monitoring of sedation depth and encephalopathy), hemodynamics and circulatory modeling (patient-specific flow simulation, peripheral waveform and pressure analysis), respiration and ventilation (ventilator waveforms and lung mechanics, gas exchange and oxygenation modeling), and deterioration and bedside monitoring (early warning scores and alarm design, sepsis prediction from streaming vital signs, multimodal waveform integration). For risk models developed on tabular record data see `health-informatics.md`; for wearables and actigraphy in daily life see `clinical-assessment.md`; for quantifying flow and function from images see `medical-imaging.md`; for electrophysiological mechanisms at the level of ion channels and cells see `references/disciplines/biology.md`.

## Screening criteria

- Build with priority: detection or classification questions on public databases for which the field has an accepted split and scoring rule. An example is heartbeat classification on the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database with a split by patient: the database holds 48 half-hour two-lead records of 47 subjects with about 110,000 beat annotations; de Chazal and colleagues divided the 44 non-paced records into two groups of 22, and on the independent test group the sensitivity for supraventricular ectopic beats was 75.9% with a positive predictive value of 38.5%, which can serve as the baseline.
- Build with priority: early-warning questions with a public scoring program and the possibility of holding out an institution. An example is predicting sepsis from hourly vital signs and laboratory results: the PhysioNet/CinC Challenge 2019 released the data of 40,336 patients from two hospital systems together with the definition of its utility score, so a model can be developed on one system with the other held out.
- Build with priority: parameter-inversion questions with a clear forward model, for which true values can be generated by simulation. An example is estimating cardiac output or arterial stiffness from the arterial pressure waveform: a public database of simulated pulse waves holds 4,374 virtual subjects whose true values come from the model, in which case the task is pure computation; public clinical records with thermodilution reference values are also available as known samples.
- Defer: tasks judged by agreement between a patient-specific flow simulation and in vivo measurement, for example a pressure gradient computed on a vessel model reconstructed from images. Boundary conditions and wall properties mostly rest on assumptions, and in vivo reference measurements are hard to obtain publicly. Discuss with the expert a formulation that fixes the geometry and boundary conditions in advance and compares with a mesh-converged reference computation or an analytical solution.
- Defer: tasks judged by the clinical effect of an alarm or early-warning system after it goes live, for example whether an early warning score reduces unplanned ICU transfers; and tasks that can be judged only with newly recorded waveforms. The former needs a prospective study and cannot be observed within six hours. The task can instead examine, on retrospective records, the missed events and false alarms per day of a fixed policy.

## Matters to confirm in conversation

- The source and recording conditions of the signal: lead or electrode configuration, sampling rate, resolution, filtering and device model, and whether the records were downsampled or lossily processed.
- The annotation guideline and granularity: per beat, per segment, per event or per record; the number of annotators and how disagreements were resolved; the standard followed (how beat classes are grouped, how seizure onset and offset are defined).
- The split: by patient, by record or by database; whether held-out data from another institution or another device exists.
- The unit of scoring and the matching rule: per sample or per event, the time tolerance for matching events, the rule for merging adjacent events, and whether false alarms are counted per hour or per day.
- The time structure of early-warning questions: the lead time of prediction, the history window available, the shift of labels relative to event onset, and whether the algorithm must be causal (using only data before the current time).
- The forward model of inversion questions and its range of validity: which parameters can be identified from the signals given, whether calibration against a reference measurement is allowed, and the error of the reference measurement itself.
- The handling of signal quality: whether records with artifacts, lead-off periods or pacing are excluded, and whether the exclusion rule is fixed in advance and applied to the held-out data in the same way.
- The form of the conclusion and the source of the tolerance: limits set by a standard of the field, agreement between annotators, or the repeatability of the reference method.

## Checking templates

The templates below add to the general templates in `references/disciplines/medicine.md`.

| Template | Criterion | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Beat-by-beat comparison | With bxb from WFDB, following the rules of ANSI/AAMI EC57, the agent judge matches the submitted beat annotations beat by beat with the reference annotations of the held-out records (default match window 150 ms) and computes sensitivity and positive predictive value for each class | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 1–2 |
| Held-out scoring by patient and across databases | The agent judge reruns the submitted model on patients that took no part in development and on records of another database, and recomputes the metrics; the drop across databases agrees with what the submitter declared | Uses existing data, must pass |
| Event-based scoring | For seizure or alarm tasks: submitted event intervals are matched with reference intervals under overlap and tolerance rules fixed in advance, and sensitivity, precision, F1 and false alarms per 24 hours are computed | Uses existing data |
| Recomputation of the utility score | From the submitted hourly predictions, the agent judge recomputes the time-dependent utility score with the public scoring program and compares it with never alerting (a score of 0) and with the baseline | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 2–3 |
| Causality audit | The agent judge truncates the input after time t and reruns; the outputs before t must stay unchanged. This confirms that filtering, normalization and imputation use no future data | Must pass |
| Agreement with a reference measurement | The estimates are compared with the reference measurement: Bland–Altman bias, limits of agreement and percentage error; trending is judged by the rate at which relative changes agree in direction | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 2 |
| Parameter recovery on simulated phantoms | With the forward model fixed in the task statement, the agent judge generates new simulated waveforms, runs the submitted inversion program, compares the recovered parameters with the true values, and confirms that a solution set or an uncertainty is given for parameters that cannot be identified | Pure computation |

## Sources of existing data

- ECG: the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database, the INCART 12-lead Arrhythmia Database (75 recordings taken from 32 Holter records), PTB-XL (21,799 12-lead ECGs of 18,869 patients, with a ten-fold split stratified by patient), the training data of the PhysioNet/CinC Challenges 2017 and 2021, and MIMIC-IV-ECG (about 800,000 diagnostic ECGs). All are under open licenses, and the ones under ODbL require a redistributed derived database to carry the same license, which has to be checked when a task package ships a reprocessed set of recordings; linking MIMIC-IV-ECG to clinical data requires the controlled-access MIMIC-IV.
- EEG and sleep: the CHB-MIT Scalp EEG Database (continuous recordings of pediatric subjects with seizure annotations, under an open license); the TUH EEG Seizure Corpus is free, but a signed request form is needed for download, and whether it may be put into a task package has to be checked against its terms of use; Sleep-EDF.
- Critical-care monitoring and surgery: the PhysioNet/CinC Challenges 2019 (sepsis) and 2015 (false ICU alarms, 750 public recordings); the MIMIC-III Waveform Database (67,830 waveform record sets of about 30,000 ICU patients, under an open license, while the matching clinical information requires the controlled-access MIMIC-III); VitalDB (intraoperative waveforms and clinical information of 6,388 surgical cases).
- Records with reference measurements: the cardiac output estimation project on PhysioNet (open access, holding the code of 11 estimators and the lists of the MIMIC-II records used; the radial arterial pressure waveforms and the thermodilution values themselves sit in the MIMIC-II waveform and numerics databases); the BIDMC dataset (53 recordings with breath-by-breath annotations from two annotators, for respiratory rate estimation).
- Simulated data: the Pulse Wave Database (pressure, flow velocity and photoplethysmogram waves at several arterial sites for 4,374 virtual subjects, released under a public-domain license). Such true values are computed from a model, so they count as pure computation, not existing data.

## Preventing answer leakage

- Beats, segments or nights of the same patient are highly similar. With a random split by beat or segment, classification metrics can be near perfect, and they fall clearly once the split is by patient. Always split by patient; segments cut by overlapping sliding windows go to the same side as a whole.
- The annotations of the classic databases are entirely public and have long been used as training data. Remove record numbers from held-out records and cut them into excerpts, and give the solving environment no network access; where possible hold out another database or the expert's own unpublished records.
- In tasks where labels are shifted relative to event onset (for example sepsis labels set positive from 6 hours before onset), variables such as ICU length of stay and the frequency of laboratory orders carry the label indirectly. The task statement states the permitted inputs, and the agent judge checks with the causality audit and by rescoring with those variables removed.
- Normalization with statistics of the whole record, zero-phase filtering or bidirectional imputation carries information from after an event into features before it. Tasks of the online early-warning kind require a causal algorithm.
- In tasks whose true values are generated by simulation, inversion is nearly trivial if the solving agent uses the same forward model and discretization that generated the data (the so-called inverse crime). The waveforms used in review are generated with a different discretization or a higher-fidelity model, with measurement noise added.

## Tools and compute

- Signal reading and processing: the WFDB Software Package and its Python implementation (including bxb and the other comparison programs that follow EC57), SciPy, NeuroKit2; for EEG and sleep, MNE-Python and YASA.
- Scoring programs: the scoring code released by the successive PhysioNet/CinC Challenges; the seizure-detection scoring library of SzCORE and its data format conventions.
- Deep learning: PyTorch, TensorFlow.
- Electrophysiology and circulation simulation: openCARP (the simulator is under an academic non-commercial license, and commercial use needs a separate license), Chaste, Myokit; SimVascular (patient-specific flow simulation).
- Compute: classical features with a classifier take minutes to hours on CPUs; training deep models on raw waveforms needs GPUs for hours to a day or two; inference and scoring again during review take minutes to tens of minutes; a single 3D electrophysiology or flow simulation already needs hours or more on a cluster, so tasks should be limited to low-dimensional models. These are rough estimates; the measurement in step 2 prevails.

## Common pitfalls

- Metrics from a random split by beat placed side by side with results from a split by patient, or a test-set score reported after repeated tuning within the same database.
- Only overall accuracy reported. Normal beats are the great majority, so the positive predictive value of minority classes such as supraventricular ectopic beats can be very low without moving the overall figure; report by class.
- Seizure detection reported by sample-wise specificity or accuracy. Seizures occupy a tiny fraction of the time, so both metrics are almost always close to 1; the scoring convention of the field is event-based sensitivity, precision and false alarms per day.
- AUROC used in place of time-dependent utility. In the sepsis challenge, on the hospital system for which no training data was provided, the utility scores of the entered algorithms were almost uncorrelated with their AUROC (Spearman ρ = 0.054).
- Development and validation on a single database. Performance falls clearly after a change of hospital, device, lead or sampling rate; resampling and filtering must be made uniform before scoring across databases, and the processing must be stated.
- The reference measurement treated as an error-free true value. Thermodilution cardiac output itself has an error of about 10%–20%, and the commonly accepted criterion for comparing a new method with the reference method is limits of agreement of up to ±30%; the tolerance cannot be tighter than the repeatability of the reference method.
- An absolute value of cardiac output estimated from a single pressure waveform without calibration. Pulse contour estimators are determined only up to a proportionality constant related to arterial compliance, and an absolute value needs a reference measurement or a population-based empirical formula.
- An inversion result taken as correct because its residual against the data is small. In an ill-posed problem several parameter sets give almost the same waveform; the result must be checked on simulated phantoms and given with an uncertainty.
- Segments with artifacts, lead-off periods or paced beats left in the scoring data with no handling agreed in advance, or excluded segments of the held-out data picked according to the results.

## Example: estimating cardiac output from the radial arterial pressure waveform

The clinical reference method for cardiac output is thermodilution through a pulmonary artery catheter, which is invasive and gives only intermittent measurements; continuous estimation of cardiac output from the arterial pressure waveform (pulse contour analysis) has been studied for about a century. Its physical basis is the Windkessel model: the ejection of each beat, acting through peripheral resistance and arterial compliance, determines the pressure waveform, and working back from the waveform yields only quantities proportional to cardiac output, with a proportionality constant that contains the compliance and resistance, which pressure alone cannot provide. Sun and colleagues evaluated 11 classical estimators on clinical waveforms from MIMIC-II: 84 patients, 988 thermodilution measurements and 165 hours of radial arterial pressure sampled at 125 Hz. With optimal calibration on all measurements, the best estimator (Liljestrand–Zander) had a standard deviation of error of 0.92 L/min, and 1.23 L/min when calibrated on the first measurement only; for relative changes without calibration its error was 18%; the simplest estimator, using mean pressure alone, also reached 1.02 L/min under optimal calibration. In one meta-analysis the pooled percentage error of pulse contour methods against thermodilution was 41.3%, short of the commonly accepted limit of 30%. The waveforms and thermodilution values of the study (records of 120 subjects) are released on PhysioNet under an open license.

The following is illustrated for the Inversion class; the class is assigned by the expert. Scientific discovery level 2, physical-world observation level 1.

- **Task statement and deliverables.** The task statement gives radial arterial pressure waveform excerpts of a number of patients (records renumbered), the value and time of each patient's first thermodilution measurement, and the times of the remaining thermodilution measurements, whose values are held out; it also gives a set of noisy simulated waveforms generated by a circulation model fixed in the task statement, with the true values held out. It provides neither the name of the study nor the published error table. The deliverables are the cardiac output estimates at each time with their uncertainties in `estimates.csv`, the parameters recovered on the simulated waveforms, a rerunnable program and a short report. The format of `estimates.csv` is the unified output format.
- **Sub-questions.** Divided by scientific content, for illustration only:
  1. Relative change: without calibration, estimate the relative change in cardiac output between the measurement times of each patient.
  2. Absolute value: calibrated on the first thermodilution measurement only, estimate cardiac output at the later times, and state the situations in which the calibration fails after the vascular state changes.
  3. Identifiability: argue which quantities a single peripheral pressure waveform determines (heart rate, the time constant of the diastolic decay, quantities proportional to stroke volume) and which are determined only up to a proportionality constant, and verify this on the simulated waveforms.
- **Given and deliverable.** The forms shown are for illustration; the expert decides which each sub-question needs. In every sub-question the given is the observed data the sub-question concerns. The deliverable is the reconstructed part, as computation (waveform features regenerated from the estimated parameters through the Windkessel model or the model used agree with the measured ones, with residuals at noise level; the true values are recovered on the simulated waveforms) and as text (the waveform features relied on: pulse pressure, systolic area, diastolic decay, and the beats rejected by the signal quality index; the argument that the proportionality constant cannot be identified, with numerical trials under different initial values and regularization). The physical constraints (positive parameters and cardiac output within the physiological range) are checked by the agent judge.
- **Checks.** The agent judge reruns the program and confirms that it reproduces the submitted estimates; it then compares them with the held-out thermodilution values and computes the standard deviation of error, the Bland–Altman bias and limits of agreement, the percentage error, and the rate at which relative changes agree in direction; on the simulated waveforms it compares the recovered parameters with the true values. Thermodilution itself has an error of about 10%–20%, so a submission whose residuals are clearly below that level is treated as fitting the noise; the tolerance is set from this error level and from the actual scores of the baseline estimators.
- **Calibration cases.** The empty submission (score 0); the same value output at every time after the first calibration (a shortcut, with acceptable absolute error and directional agreement of relative changes at chance); the estimator using mean pressure alone (the baseline); the Liljestrand–Zander estimator (a known result); a submission with correct relative changes whose absolute values are shifted as a whole by a wrong proportionality constant (partially correct); a submission whose estimates match the held-out values point by point, with residuals far below the error of thermodilution, while the program cannot reproduce them (fitting the noise or taken from the public values, score 0).
- **Other tasks of the same structure.** Estimating the resistance and compliance of the respiratory system from airway pressure and flow waveforms during passive ventilation; reconstructing epicardial potentials from body-surface potentials. The checks on the forward model, the simulated phantoms and identifiability stay the same.
