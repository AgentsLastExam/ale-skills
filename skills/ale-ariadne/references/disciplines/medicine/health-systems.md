# Medicine: health systems and implementation

This file adds content specific to health systems and implementation to `references/disciplines/medicine.md`, whose provisions still apply.

Scope: quality improvement and patient safety (measuring adverse events, improvement cycles and control charts), implementation science (de-implementation of low-value care), primary and community care (continuity, access and panel management), workforce and staffing (measuring workload from EHR audit logs, nurse rostering and staffing optimization), and care coordination and referral (discharge, readmission and handoff, referral pathways and triage). For payment methods and cost-effectiveness see `health-policy-economics.md`; for the design of causal inference from observational data see `epidemiology.md`; for the development and calibration of risk prediction models such as readmission models see `health-informatics.md`; for the design of cluster-randomized and stepped-wedge trials see `clinical-trials.md`.

## Screening criteria

- Build with priority: rostering and staffing optimization problems whose constraints and objective function can be checked item by item by a program. An example is nurse rostering on public benchmark instances: whether the hard constraints hold and what the penalty is can be recomputed exactly, and proven optima or best known solutions exist to compare against.
- Build with priority: capacity and flow models that can be rerun once the inputs are fixed. An example is bed capacity for emergency admissions: the discrete-event simulation of Bagust and colleagues found that the risk of having no bed becomes discernible once average occupancy exceeds about 85%, and that bed shortages are regular at 90% or more; analytical results of queueing theory serve as a comparison, and the agent judge can rerun the simulation.
- Build with priority: questions that recompute quality indicators on public institution-level data and identify special-cause variation, for example a funnel plot of public hospital readmission or mortality indicators, or a control chart of a monthly series. The rules are fixed in advance, and the conclusions can be checked point by point.
- Defer: questions in which the effect of a regulation or intervention can be judged only after years of observation, for example whether mandated nurse-to-patient ratios lower mortality, or whether the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program of US Medicare (which since October 2012 has reduced payments on the basis of 30-day readmission measures for six conditions, by up to 3%) lowered readmissions without raising mortality. Patient-level data is under controlled access, and the conclusions vary with how coding changes and regression to the mean are handled. Discuss with the expert building only the sub-problem that can be computed: rostering optimization under given demand and constraints, or an interrupted time series analysis with the model specification fixed in advance; the sub-problem does not show the effect of the regulation itself.
- Defer: questions in which the effectiveness of an implementation or de-implementation strategy can be judged only by a new stepped-wedge trial or field observation (physical-world observation level 3); workload measurement that depends on an institution's internal EHR audit logs, which cannot be put into a task package. The former can become a reanalysis of public trial data, or a check of the statistical power of a design by simulation; the latter can use de-identified aggregate data provided by the expert.

## Matters to confirm in conversation

- The unit and level of analysis (patient, physician, ward, hospital), and how the clustering enters the model.
- The operational definition of the indicator: numerator, denominator, exclusions and time window (for example, whether 30-day unplanned readmission counts readmissions to other hospitals and excludes planned readmissions), and the risk adjustment model and its version.
- In rostering and staffing problems, the division between hard and soft constraints and the source of each (regulation, contract, ward custom), who sets the penalty weights, and where the demand data comes from.
- In simulation models, what the arrival process and service time distributions rest on, the warm-up period, run length and number of replications, and the historical indicators against which the model is validated.
- In improvement or implementation studies, the content of the intervention, the implementation strategy and the context, whether the start of the intervention was fixed in advance, and whether other changes occurred at the same time.
- For control charts and funnel plots, the chart type, the baseline period, the control limits and the rules, and whether overdispersion is corrected.
- The form of the conclusion (an optimum or a bound, an improvement over a baseline, the presence of special-cause variation) and what the tolerance rests on (exact recomputation, variation between random seeds, the confidence interval of a simulation).
- How the objective function or indicator relates to the real quality of care; the expert states how far it is representative and where its limits lie.

## Checking templates

The templates below add to the general templates in `references/disciplines/medicine.md`.

| Template | Criterion | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Feasibility of the roster and recomputation of the objective | The agent judge calls a numerical-check script written independently, which checks every hard constraint of the submitted roster and recomputes the penalty; an instance that violates any hard constraint scores nothing, and the values the submitter reports are not used | Pure computation, must pass |
| Gap to the optimum or the lower bound | The relative gap of the recomputed objective to the proven optimum or the best lower bound, averaged over the instances under a fixed time limit and fixed hardware | Pure computation, scientific discovery level 2–3 |
| Rerun on held-out instances | The agent judge reruns the submitted solver under the same time limit on unpublished instances of the same kind; the gap is comparable to that on the public instances | Pure computation, scientific discovery level 2–3 |
| Validity of lower bounds | A submitted lower bound may not exceed the objective of any known feasible solution; where a dual solution or certificate is attached, the agent judge verifies it again | Pure computation, counts for part of the score |
| Rerun of the simulation model | The submitted queueing or discrete-event simulation model is rerun with fixed inputs and random seeds: occupancy, waiting-time quantiles and the probability of rejection fall within the confidence interval of the reference simulation, and limiting cases agree with the analytical results of queueing theory | Pure computation |
| Control chart and funnel plot signals | The center line and control limits are recomputed from the public or given indicator series, and the special-cause signals marked in the submission agree point by point with the reference analysis | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 1–2 |
| Re-estimation of an interrupted time series | With a segmented regression specification fixed in advance, the level change and slope change are re-estimated with their confidence intervals, and residual autocorrelation and placebo time points are checked | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 2 |

## Sources of existing data

- Rostering benchmark instances: the 24 nurse rostering instances maintained by Curtois and colleagues (2 to 52 weeks, 8 to 150 staff), whose website publishes lower bounds, best known solutions and the roster files; the instances of the two International Nurse Rostering Competitions, INRC-I and INRC-II, the latter with a validator; NSPLib. The optima are computed, so they count as pure computation, not existing data.
- Institution-level quality indicators: the hospital-level indicators that CMS publishes in its data catalog, including the results of the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program; the emergency waiting-time statistics and the hospital-level mortality indicator SHMI published by the NHS in England. All are data aggregated by institution.
- Patient-level readmission and emergency data: the Nationwide Readmissions Database of HCUP must be purchased and requires a signed data use agreement; MIMIC-IV-ED requires completed training and a signed agreement and cannot be redistributed; NHAMCS is public sample survey data; the Diabetes 130-US hospitals dataset at UCI is public and carries a 30-day readmission label.
- Synthetic data: the synthetic claims public-use file of CMS, DE-SynPUF, can be used to rehearse a workflow and does not support substantive inference.
- Audit logs and rostering records are mostly internal to an institution and usually may not leave it; in a task they are replaced by simulated data or by de-identified aggregate data provided by the expert.

## Preventing answer leakage

- The optimal rosters of the public benchmark instances can be downloaded directly. Make the submitted solver the object of scoring: the agent judge reruns it on unpublished held-out instances, and the objective is recomputed by the numerical-check script.
- The held-out instances are generated by the expert in the same format (with changed demand, leave requests and contract parameters); their reference optima are computed in step 2 and kept only in `tests/`.
- The solving environment has no network access, and the task statement contains no instance numbers or benchmark names; a program that looks up answers by instance name fails on the held-out instances.
- The official ratings of public indicators and published evaluation conclusions can be found by searching. Switch to a period or a subset of institutions the papers do not cover, and require analysis code that can be rerun.

## Tools and compute

- Mathematical programming and constraint solving: OR-Tools (CP-SAT), HiGHS, SCIP (open source); Gurobi and CPLEX need a commercial license, with academic licenses available.
- Modeling interfaces and heuristic solving: Pyomo, PuLP, MiniZinc; Timefold Solver.
- Queueing and discrete-event simulation: SimPy and Ciw (Python), simmer (R).
- Statistics for quality improvement: in R, qicharts2 (run charts and control charts) and FunnelPlotR (funnel plots); segmented regression uses general statistical software.
- Compute: the convention of rostering benchmarks is a single thread and a time limit of minutes to one hour per instance, so solving all instances is on the order of tens of core-hours; checking one roster takes seconds, and rerunning the solver on held-out instances during review is of the same order as solving. These are rough estimates; the measurement in step 2 prevails.

## Common pitfalls

- A division between hard and soft constraints that differs from the benchmark, so the objectives cannot be compared. In INRC-II, staffing below the minimum requirement is a hard constraint and staffing below the optimal requirement is a soft one.
- The convention at the borders of the planning horizon ignored. Whether the days outside the horizon are treated as days off or continue from the history of the previous planning period differs between benchmarks.
- The objective the submitter reports taken at face value, or the numerical-check script and the solver sharing the same code, so that errors hide each other.
- Methods compared without fixing the time limit, the number of threads and the hardware, or only the best of several random runs reported.
- Parameters tuned to the public instances, which then fail on the held-out instances.
- An improvement in the benchmark objective stated as an improvement in quality of care or in nurse workload. The penalty weights are only a proxy for the preferences of a ward.
- A simulation with no warm-up period or too few replications, or the risk of having no bed at peak times inferred from average occupancy.
- A control chart whose baseline period includes data from after the intervention; institutions compared without correcting for overdispersion, so that many are flagged as outliers.
- A before-and-after comparison that leaves regression to the mean, seasonality and concurrent changes unhandled. A fall in readmission rates can come partly from changes in diagnosis coding and not from changes in care.

## Example: nurse rostering optimization on public benchmark instances

Nurse staffing is associated with patient outcomes: the 2002 cross-sectional study by Aiken and colleagues of 168 hospitals and more than 230,000 surgical patients in Pennsylvania found that each additional patient per nurse raised the odds of death within 30 days of admission by 7%. Which staffing levels and rostering patterns improve outcomes can be answered only by years of observation and policy experiments, and cannot be judged here. What can be built is the computational sub-problem within it: find the roster of least penalty under given demand, contracts and rest rules. In 2014 Curtois and Qu reported computational results on 24 benchmark instances, ranging from 2 weeks, 8 staff and 1 shift type to 52 weeks, 150 staff and 32 shift types; at the time a general integer programming solver run on a single thread for one hour gave no feasible solution for the five largest instances, and a heuristic run for one hour reached an objective of 156,858 on the largest. Methods have improved steadily since: the optima of 23 instances are now proven, and the largest instance has a best known solution of 40,439 against a lower bound of 40,438. The task asks the solving agent to develop a solution method that comes as close to the optimum as possible within a fixed time limit; the result shows only how good the method is, not which staffing is safer for patients.

The following is illustrated for the Development class; the class is assigned by the expert. Scientific discovery level 2, physical-world observation level 0.

- **Task statement and deliverables.** The task statement gives the problem definition, the instance file format, several development instances, the time limit and the hardware specification. The hard constraints are at most one shift per day, shift rotation, the maximum number of shifts of each type, minimum and maximum work time, minimum and maximum consecutive shifts, minimum consecutive days off, the maximum number of weekends worked and fixed days off; the objective is the weighted sum of penalties for unmet shift-on requests, violated shift-off requests, and staffing below or above the requirement of each shift. The task statement gives no optimum and no known roster for any instance. The deliverables are the source code of the solver, a roster file for each development instance, and a `results.json` collecting objectives, lower bounds and run times. The format of the roster file is the unified output format.
- **Sub-questions.** Divided by scientific content, for illustration only:
  1. Decomposition of the problem: column generation in which a column is the individual roster of one nurse, and the pricing problem is a shortest path problem with resource constraints.
  2. Search for feasible solutions: neighborhood structures (swaps of shifts over several consecutive days and chained moves) and large neighborhood search.
  3. Lower bounds and optimality: the lower bound given by the linear relaxation and the branching strategy, and the gap between the lower bound and the feasible solution.
- **Given and deliverable.** The forms shown are for illustration; the expert decides which each sub-question needs. In every sub-question the given is the part of the existing method the sub-question concerns, with the metric and the constraints to be maintained. The deliverable is the improvement and its contribution to the metric, as text (why the improvement narrows the gap, and why the hard constraints still hold) and as computation (the improved solver and its objectives and gaps; the gap with the improvement switched off, which is the ablation and establishes its contribution). The agent judge reruns the solver and recomputes objectives and gaps.
- **Checks.** The numerical-check script verifies every hard constraint and recomputes the penalty; the objective is an integer and must agree exactly with the value the submitter reports. The metric is the mean relative gap to the reference optimum over the instances, under a fixed time limit and a single thread; the agent judge reruns the solver on the held-out instances with three random seeds and takes the median. The baseline is the gap of a general integer programming solver under the same time limit, measured in step 2. A submitted lower bound above the reference optimum makes that item fail. The tolerance on the gap is set from the variation of one program between seeds observed in step 2, and differences in timing are controlled by fixing the hardware and the number of threads.
- **Tiers and scoring.** Instances of up to 4 weeks form the first tier, instances of 6 to 12 weeks the second, and instances of 26 weeks or more the third; completing only the first tier earns the corresponding score. The score follows the position of the gap between the baseline and the optimum, and the mapping is set by the expert.
- **Calibration cases.** The empty submission (score 0); a program that outputs downloaded optimal rosters by instance name and finds no solution on the held-out instances (a shortcut; the held-out instances score 0); a roster that reaches a lower objective by relaxing the shift rotation or minimum rest constraints (a shortcut; the numerical-check script finds it infeasible); a feasible roster built greedily (partially correct); the result of a general integer programming solver under the same time limit (the baseline); the proven optima of the public instances (a known result).
- **Other tasks of the same structure.** Switch to the instances of INRC-II (originally solved week by week, with history passed from one week to the next; the literature mostly studies the static version in which all information is given in advance): a branch-and-price study of 2023 proved the optimality of all 4-week instances and of 40% of the 8-week instances of the static version, and the instances not yet proven can serve as tasks of scientific discovery level 3; or change to operating room scheduling or emergency physician rostering.
