# Medicine: clinical trials and evidence generation

This file adds content specific to clinical trials and evidence generation to `references/disciplines/medicine.md`, whose provisions still apply.

Scope: randomized trial design (randomization, blinding and allocation concealment, non-inferiority and equivalence designs, pragmatic and registry-based trials), adaptive and platform trials (interim analysis and stopping rules, master protocols, Bayesian adaptive randomization), real-world evidence and external controls (external and synthetic control arms, fitness for purpose of real-world data), endpoints and patient-reported outcomes (validation of surrogate endpoints, development and scoring of instruments, composite endpoints and the win ratio), and the conduct, monitoring and analysis of trials (statistical analysis plans and estimands, missing data and intercurrent events, safety monitoring). For estimating causal effects from observational data see `epidemiology.md`; for pharmacokinetics and exposure–response analysis see `therapeutics-pharmacology.md`; for the psychometric properties of instruments see `clinical-assessment.md`; for cluster-randomized field trials see `global-environmental-health.md`.

## Screening criteria

- Build with priority: questions in which the properties of a design and analysis method can be judged by simulation. An example is a framework for confirming efficacy when the worldwide patient population is only 10–30, or a single patient: how N-of-1 crossover designs, external natural-history controls and Bayesian dynamic borrowing are combined, measured by type I error, power, bias and coverage, all of it pure computation that needs no patient data.
- Build with priority: questions that ask for the operating characteristics of a design with given parameters, for example the stopping boundaries and expected sample size of a group sequential design, the sample size under a non-inferiority margin, or the error rates of a platform trial with a shared control arm. Such quantities have analytic solutions, or results from established software such as `rpact` and `gsDesign`, to compare against.
- Build with priority: questions that reanalyze public participant-level data under an estimand written in advance, for example comparing, in the public database of the International Stroke Trial, the conclusions when the functional outcome at 6 months is analyzed as an ordered scale (a proportional odds model or the win ratio) and when it is dichotomized as "dead or dependent". The data is public and the analysis plan can be fixed in advance.
- Defer: trial-level validation of a surrogate endpoint, for example predicting the effect of treatment on overall survival from its effect on progression-free survival. This needs participant-level data from several randomized trials, which mostly requires an application and cannot be redistributed. Discuss with the expert switching to public multicenter trial data with the center as the unit, or examining only individual-level association and mediation within a single trial, stating that the analysis does not show trial-level surrogacy.
- Defer: questions that can be judged only by newly recruited participants or by waiting for follow-up, for example the primary endpoint result of an ongoing trial (physical-world observation level 3); questions scored on whether a regulatory agency approves a drug or accepts an endpoint.

## Matters to confirm in conversation

- The attributes of the estimand (in the framework of ICH E9(R1)): population, treatment, endpoint variable, the strategy for intercurrent events and the population-level summary; which assumption each sensitivity analysis addresses.
- The convention on error rates: one-sided 0.025 or another level, for a single hypothesis or a family of hypotheses; whether a Bayesian design must be calibrated to the frequentist type I error, or whether other performance metrics are used with the expert's agreement.
- Who sets the range of simulation scenarios: effect size, variability, dropout, time trend, and the degree of conflict between the external data and the current trial; which scenarios are disclosed to the solving agent and which are held out.
- The source of the external control or historical data, whether its eligibility criteria, endpoint definition and timing of measurement match the current trial, and the cap on borrowing (prior effective sample size).
- N-of-1 and crossover designs: the number of periods and the washout, the assumptions on carryover and time trend, the autocorrelation structure of the outcome, and whether the target is the individual effect or the average effect; whether the report is held to the CONSORT extension for N-of-1 trials (CENT 2015).
- Surrogate endpoints: the level of validation (individual-level association or trial-level association), the number of trials it rests on, and the class of interventions and the population to which the conclusion is limited.
- The minimal clinically important difference, the non-inferiority margin, and what they rest on.
- The form of the conclusion (a design with its operating characteristics, an effect estimate, a judgement on whether a marker is a surrogate endpoint), and what the tolerance rests on: the Monte Carlo standard error of the simulation, the confidence interval of the reference analysis.

## Checking templates

The templates below add to the general templates in `references/disciplines/medicine.md`.

| Template | Criterion | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Performance measures on held-out scenarios | The agent judge reruns the submitted design and analysis code on scenarios not disclosed in the task statement, with new random seeds: power, bias of the estimate and interval coverage under scenarios with an effect are scored by the margin over the baseline method; the number of repetitions per scenario must make the Monte Carlo standard error smaller than the tolerance | Pure computation, scientific discovery level 2–3 |
| Error-rate control | Under every scenario with no effect (including those in which the external data conflicts with the current data), the rejection rate does not exceed the limit fixed in advance plus the tolerance; on failure, the power item scores nothing | Pure computation, must pass |
| Comparison with analytic results or established software | Group sequential boundaries, sample size and expected sample size agree, within numerical precision, with the analytic solution or with the results of `rpact` and `gsDesign` | Pure computation, scientific discovery level 1 |
| Sensitivity analysis under the stated estimand | Given the data and the analysis plan, the agent judge reruns the submitted code: the estimates from analyses that change the assumed missingness mechanism (reference-based imputation, tipping-point analysis), and the point at which the conclusion reverses, agree with the reference analysis | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 1–2 |
| Discrimination of control markers | Control markers of known structure are mixed into the data (prognostic only, responsive to the intervention but not mediating, truly mediating), and the submitted analysis must classify them correctly; for the real markers, each effect and the proportion mediated agree with the reference analysis within its interval | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 2–3 |
| Trial-level surrogacy | Where several trials or several centers are available, the effect of treatment on the outcome in held-out units is predicted from the effect of treatment on the marker, and the observed value falls within the prediction interval; the trial-level R² is reported with its interval | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 2–3 |

## Sources of existing data

- Public participant-level data: the International Stroke Trial database (19,435 patients with acute stroke); the example data from multicenter trials shipped with the R package `Surrogate` (age-related macular degeneration, advanced ovarian cancer, schizophrenia), widely used to demonstrate methods for surrogate endpoints.
- Participant-level data that needs an application: BioLINCC of NHLBI and the NCTN/NCORP trial data of NCI (released through dbGaP since May 2025); natural-history and historical-control data for rare diseases, for example PRO-ACT (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, pooled de-identified records from many phase II and III trials) and RDCA-DAP of C-Path, all of which need an application and agreement to terms of use. Whether the data may be put into a task package must be checked agreement by agreement.
- Aggregate data: the results data of ClinicalTrials.gov and its relational database version AACT (public); the table published by FDA of surrogate endpoints that were the basis of an approval.
- Trials of interventions on aging: CALERIE (a randomized trial of two years of caloric restriction, with DNA methylation data at baseline, 12 months and 24 months) is under controlled access through the Aging Research Biobank of NIA.
- Simulation benchmarks: in tasks on ultra-small samples, adaptive designs and borrowing of external information, the data is produced by a data-generating mechanism fixed in advance and the true values are known, so the task counts as pure computation, not existing data. The data-generating mechanism can be set from public natural-history data or from the summary statistics of published trials.

## Preventing answer leakage

- The parameters, random seeds and data-generating code of the held-out scenarios are kept only in `tests/`, and the task statement describes only the classes of scenario and the parameter ranges. The disclosed development scenarios and the held-out scenarios do not share parameter values, so that thresholds cannot be tuned to scenarios already seen.
- The most common shortcut in such tasks is to relax the error rate in exchange for power: borrowing all of the external data, choosing the analysis method after the fact, or looking repeatedly at interim data without adjusting the boundaries. The agent judge reruns the code and does not use the operating characteristics the submitter reports; the type I error is a condition that must pass.
- The tables of simulation results in methodological papers can be found by searching. Switch to sample sizes, prior parameters and conflict scenarios that the papers did not report, or ask for quantities they did not report (the curve of type I error as the conflict grows step by step, the prior effective sample size).
- In a task on surrogate endpoints, the conclusion for a real marker may already be in the literature. Mix into the data control markers generated by the reviewing side, whose class cannot be found by searching; the score is determined mainly by the discrimination of the control markers and by recomputed quantities.

## Tools and compute

- Design and simulation: in R, `rpact` and `gsDesign` (group sequential and adaptive designs), `rsimsum` (summary of simulation results with Monte Carlo standard errors); FACTS and East are commercial software.
- Borrowing of external information: `RBesT` (robust MAP priors), `psborrow2` (commensurate priors, depends on cmdstan), `SAMprior`; general Bayesian modeling uses Stan and `brms`.
- Estimands and missing data: `mmrm`, `rbmi` (reference-based multiple imputation); the win ratio for composite endpoints uses `WINS`.
- Surrogate endpoints and mediation analysis: `Surrogate`, `mediation`.
- Compute: for one scenario repeated ten thousand times, a frequentist analysis takes a few core-minutes and a Bayesian analysis that needs MCMC takes tens to hundreds of core-hours; a full evaluation over tens of scenarios has to run in parallel on a multi-core machine or a cluster. The rerun on held-out scenarios during review costs about as much as one round of evaluation during solving, and can be kept down by using analytic posteriors under conjugate priors or by reducing the number of scenarios. These are rough estimates; the measurement in step 2 prevails.

## Common pitfalls

- Performance measures reported only for scenarios in which the external data agrees with the current data. With dynamic borrowing the inflation of type I error is largest at a moderate degree of conflict, so the scenarios must cover conflict that grows step by step.
- A claim of a power gain under strict control of type I error. Kopp-Schneider and colleagues proved that when a uniformly most powerful test exists and the external information is conditioned on, strict control of type I error rules out any power gain, dynamic borrowing included. The task must state the permitted error-rate limit and the range of scenarios, and what the score measures is this trade-off.
- Too few repetitions. With a type I error of 0.025 and ten thousand repetitions the Monte Carlo standard error is about 0.0016; a tighter tolerance cannot be judged.
- Carryover, time trend and autocorrelation of the outcome ignored in N-of-1 and crossover designs; a treatment with an irreversible effect (gene editing, for example) placed in a crossover design.
- An external control that differs from the trial population in eligibility criteria, endpoint measurement and start of follow-up, with the difference counted as treatment effect.
- A prognostic marker taken for a surrogate endpoint. In CAST, encainide and flecainide suppressed ventricular premature beats, yet total mortality was higher than with placebo (7.7% against 3.0%).
- The "proportion of treatment effect explained" within a single trial used as the ground for accepting a surrogate endpoint. The proportion is highly unstable when the treatment effect is small, and it is confounded by common causes of the marker and the outcome.
- The endpoint, sample size or analysis method changed after an interim analysis without being counted in the error rate; in a non-inferiority trial, poor adherence makes the arms converge, so that the intention-to-treat analysis leans toward concluding non-inferiority.
- An estimand that does not state the strategy for intercurrent events, so that different analyses answer different questions while their values are compared directly.

## Example: regulatory-grade efficacy confirmation in ultra-small samples

Gene therapies, ultra-rare genetic disorders and ever finer molecular subtypes of cancer are turning more and more indications into ones with only 10–30 eligible patients worldwide, or a single patient; the in vivo base-editing therapy customized for one infant and reported in 2025 is the case of a sample size of 1. Restricted randomization, repeated-measures N-of-1 crossover designs, external natural-history controls and Bayesian dynamic borrowing each have their own methods, and in January 2026 FDA issued a draft guidance on the use of Bayesian methods in clinical trials, but there is no consensus on how to combine them so that the quantification of uncertainty withstands regulatory scrutiny. The north-star problem is an evidentiary framework that generalizes across diseases, is sufficient to support approval and is replicated in at least three distinct indications; whether regulatory agencies adopt it cannot be judged here. What can be built is the purely methodological sub-problem within it: propose a design and analysis framework and test its type I error, power, bias and coverage on a simulated trial benchmark, under scenarios the solving agent has not seen.

The following is illustrated for the Development class; the class is assigned by the expert. Scientific discovery level 3, physical-world observation level 0.

- **Task statement and deliverables.** The task statement describes several classes of simulated trial scenario in ultra-rare disease and provides instances for development: a total of 10–30 patients or 1, the type of outcome (continuous repeated measures, binary), the external natural-history data available, and the limit on the one-sided type I error; the parameters of the held-out scenarios (effect size, degree of conflict of the external data, time trend, carryover) are not disclosed. The deliverables are runnable code for the design and analysis (it takes the data-generating interface of a scenario and returns, for each simulation run, whether the null hypothesis is rejected, the effect estimate and its interval), a description of the design and a short report. The results table with one row per simulation run is the unified output format.
- **Sub-questions.** Divided by scientific content, for illustration only:
  1. Within-patient comparison: the randomization scheme when a treatment with a reversible effect is studied in an N-of-1 or multi-period crossover design, and the handling of carryover and time trend.
  2. Borrowing of external information: a control constructed from natural-history data, with the strength of borrowing adjusted to the agreement between the current data and the external data.
  3. Evidence synthesis and the decision rule: combining the within-patient comparison and the external borrowing into one efficacy conclusion, with the way its error rate is calibrated.
- **Given and deliverable.** The forms shown are for illustration; the expert decides which each sub-question needs. In every sub-question the given is the part of the existing method the sub-question concerns, with the metric and the constraints to be maintained. The deliverable is the improvement and its contribution to the metric, as text (why this component raises power, and why the error rate and coverage remain under control) and as computation (the code of the component and its metrics; the change in the metrics when the component is removed and replaced by a baseline practice, for example no borrowing, full borrowing, borrowing with a fixed weight, or ignoring carryover, which is the ablation). The agent judge reruns the code and recomputes every metric.
- **Checks.** The agent judge runs the submitted code on the held-out scenarios with new random seeds. Under scenarios with no effect (including those in which the external data conflicts with the current data) the rejection rate must not exceed the stated limit plus the tolerance, which is a condition that must pass; once it passes, the score follows the gain in power over the baseline (an analysis of the current data alone) under scenarios with an effect, the bias of the estimate and the coverage of the 95% interval. With ten thousand repetitions per scenario, the Monte Carlo standard error of a type I error of 0.025 is about 0.0016, and the tolerance is two to three times that; the number of repetitions and the tolerance are provisional and are set from the trial runs in step 2.
- **Calibration cases.** The empty submission (score 0); an analysis that borrows all of the external data, with very high power when the data agree and uncontrolled type I error when they conflict (a shortcut: a higher metric that violates the constraint); a method whose thresholds are tuned to the disclosed instances and that fails on the held-out scenarios (a shortcut); an analysis of the current data alone (the baseline); a published dynamic borrowing method such as the robust MAP prior (a known result, partially correct).
- **What passing does not show.** The checks are a proxy verifier and test necessary conditions only: passing shows that, within the family of scenarios set, the framework keeps the error rate under control and does better than the baseline. It does not show that the family of scenarios covers the natural history and the kinds of conflict of real diseases, that a regulatory agency would accept this convention on the error rate, or that the framework has been replicated in three or more real indications. These remain for the expert to judge.

## Example: is an aging marker prognostic or a surrogate endpoint

The north-star problem is to find a biomarker of aging that an intervention can modify, whose longitudinal change prospectively predicts all-cause mortality, that is validated in at least two large independent cohorts and that regulatory agencies accept as a primary endpoint, so that interventions on aging can be evaluated within three years instead of thirty. At present aging itself is not an approved indication and there is no validated biomarker of aging; the TAME trial (metformin, planned for more than 3,000 participants aged 65–79 followed for six years), which works around this with a composite endpoint of several age-related diseases, has no primary endpoint data yet. Regulatory acceptance and prospective mortality cannot be judged here. The sub-problem that can be built is the distinction between a prognostic marker and a surrogate endpoint: the former only predicts risk, while the latter must also change with treatment, and that change must reflect the downstream clinical benefit. The CALERIE trial gives an instance: two years of caloric restriction slowed the pace of aging measured by DunedinPACE by 2–3%, whereas PhenoAge and GrimAge showed no significant change.

The following is illustrated for the Explanation class; the class is assigned by the expert. Scientific discovery level 3, physical-world observation level 1.

- **Task statement and deliverables.** The task statement gives de-identified participant-level data from a randomized intervention trial (several candidate markers at baseline and follow-up, downstream outcomes, covariates), mixed with control markers planted by the reviewing side; the solving agent does not know which are planted. The data must be a version the expert may compliantly put into a task; the molecular data of CALERIE is under controlled access and cannot be packaged directly. The deliverables are a `results.json` with the classification of each marker and every effect estimate with its interval, the analysis code and a short report; the format of `results.json` is the unified output format.
- **Sub-questions.** Divided by scientific content, for illustration only:
  1. Prognostic value: whether the baseline marker predicts the outcome in the control arm.
  2. Response to the intervention: whether the intervention changes the marker, and by how much.
  3. Mediation: what proportion of the effect of the intervention on the outcome passes through the change in the marker.
- **Given and deliverable.** The forms shown are for illustration; the expert decides which each sub-question needs. The given is the phenomenon (the effect of the intervention on the marker and on the outcome, with its size, as present in the trial data). The deliverable is the account of how far the marker carries that effect, as computation (the mediation model, whose ablation is the change in the intervention effect when the path through the marker is blocked, that is, the decomposition into direct and indirect effects) and as text (the set of assumptions under which the decomposition holds, namely no unmeasured confounding between marker and outcome and correct temporal order, with the corresponding sensitivity analysis).
- **Checks.** The agent judge reruns the submitted code: correct classification of the planted control markers is a condition that must pass; for the real markers, the effects of the intervention on the marker and on the outcome and the proportion mediated differ from the reference analysis by no more than twice its standard error. The tolerance is provisional and is set from the spread observed under resampling in step 2.
- **Calibration cases.** The empty submission (score 0); every marker associated with the outcome called a surrogate endpoint (a shortcut; the prognostic-only control markers are misclassified); a submission that completes only the first two sub-questions (partially correct); the reference analysis itself (a known result).
- **What passing does not show.** Passing shows only that, in one trial, for one intervention and one intermediate outcome, the change in the marker statistically mediates the effect of the intervention. It does not show that the relation holds under other interventions (trial-level surrogacy needs several trials), that a change in the marker predicts mortality, or that a regulatory agency would accept the marker as an endpoint. These are for the expert to judge, and in the end for prospective studies and regulatory review to decide.
