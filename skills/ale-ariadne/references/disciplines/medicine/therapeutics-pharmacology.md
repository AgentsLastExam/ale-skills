# Medicine: therapeutics and pharmacology

This file adds content specific to therapeutics and pharmacology to `references/disciplines/medicine.md`, whose provisions still apply.

Scope: pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (population pharmacokinetics and covariate modeling, physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling, exposure–response analysis), dose individualization (therapeutic drug monitoring, dosing in children, pregnancy and organ impairment), drug safety and pharmacovigilance (signal detection in spontaneous reports, drug–drug interactions and polypharmacy), resistance and combination therapy (combination design and synergy), and formulation and delivery (route of administration, bioavailability and adherence). For genotype-guided prescribing see `precision-medicine.md`; for antimicrobial stewardship and resistance outcomes see `infectious-disease.md`; for trial design and endpoints see `clinical-trials.md`; for estimating drug effects from observational data see `epidemiology.md`; for the binding of drug molecules to their targets see `references/disciplines/chemistry.md`.

## Screening criteria

- Build with priority: questions in which the prediction of a mechanistic model can be compared with published clinical observations. An example is predicting, with a physiologically based pharmacokinetic model, the effect of CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers on midazolam exposure: the open-source platform and the modeling workflow are mature, the AUC ratios of clinical interaction studies exist to compare against, and the field has accepted acceptance limits (the twofold range and the Guest criterion).
- Build with priority: questions that build a population pharmacokinetic model on a public dataset and evaluate it on held-out subjects, for example the clearance of warfarin, or of phenobarbital in neonates, with its covariate model. The data is public and the workflow of nonlinear mixed-effects modeling is standard; subjects must be held out and the evaluation metrics fixed in advance, otherwise the task reduces to reproducing textbook parameters.
- Build with priority: disproportionality signal detection in a spontaneous-report database, evaluated against a public reference set. An example is computing signal metrics for drug–event pairs from the quarterly FAERS data and comparing with the OMOP reference set (four outcomes, 165 positive controls and 234 negative controls), or with the time-indexed reference set compiled from the labeling revisions of 2013 (62 positive and 75 negative). The data and the reference sets are public, and the latter allows a split by time.
- Defer: questions that can be judged only by a new in vitro experiment or newly collected clinical samples, for example the exposure of an investigational drug in patients with hepatic impairment, or the synergy of a new combination. For combination questions, discuss with the expert a prediction task that uses only published combination screening data (such as NCI-ALMANAC) and is evaluated on held-out drug pairs.
- Defer: tasks whose answer is a dosing regimen for one patient, for example a dose adjustment recommended from one patient's plasma concentrations. Discuss with the expert evaluating the dosing algorithm instead, by its concentration prediction error and probability of target attainment on public data or on virtual patients simulated from a fixed population model, with no advice given for an individual.

## Matters to confirm in conversation

- The structure and level of the model: compartmental or physiological; the system parameters fixed in advance (physiology database, enzyme abundance) and the drug parameters to be estimated; the platform and version used.
- Population and dosing regimen: dose, route, dosing interval, and the timing of the two drugs relative to each other; healthy volunteers or patients; the ranges of age, body weight, organ function and genotype.
- Where the mechanistic interaction parameters come from (Ki and kinact measured in vitro, EC50 and Emax of induction, the fraction metabolized fm by the enzyme) and their uncertainty; which may be calibrated on clinical data, and which studies must be kept for verification.
- The analyte and the matrix (total or unbound concentration, plasma or whole blood), and how data below the limit of quantification is handled.
- The convention on evaluation metrics and limits: whether AUC ratios and Cmax ratios are judged by the twofold range or by the Guest criterion; whether a population model is judged by prediction error, npde or a visual predictive check; whether the field accepts this limit.
- Pharmacovigilance: how drug names are normalized (RxNorm, ATC), the level of the event terminology (MedDRA preferred terms or standardized queries), whether only primary suspect drugs or all drugs are counted, the deduplication rule and the background comparison set; whether the report is held to READUS-PV.
- The source and cutoff date of the reference set, and what the positive and negative controls rest on.
- The form of the conclusion (a ratio with its interval, parameter estimates, a ranking of signals), and what the tolerance rests on: the variability between clinical studies, the relative standard errors of the parameters, the size of the reference set.

## Checking templates

The templates below add to the general templates in `references/disciplines/medicine.md`.

| Template | Criterion | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Prediction of interaction ratios | The agent judge reruns the submitted model to obtain the AUC ratio and Cmax ratio under the conditions of the held-out clinical studies and compares them with the observed values: the fraction falling within the limits of the Guest criterion, and the geometric mean fold error | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 2 |
| Single-drug models verified first | For the victim drug and the perpetrator drug each given alone, the simulated AUC and Cmax are within twofold of the observed values; on failure, the interaction predictions score nothing | Uses existing data, must pass |
| Evaluation of a population model on held-out subjects | The agent judge uses the submitted model to make population and individual predictions for the held-out subjects: the median prediction error (bias), the median absolute prediction error (precision), and the distribution test of npde | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 2–3 |
| Stability of the parameter estimates | The agent judge refits the model: the objective function value agrees with the value the submitter reports, and different initial values converge to the same solution; the relative standard errors and the shrinkage of the parameters are within the agreed range | Must pass |
| Mass balance and physiological constraints | In a physiological model the organ blood flows sum to cardiac output, the tissue volumes are consistent with body weight, and the dose is conserved across compartments and routes of elimination | Pure computation, must pass |
| Discrimination in signal detection | The agent judge uses the submitted code to recompute the signal metrics on reports dated before the cutoff, computes the AUROC and the sensitivity at a fixed specificity from the positive and negative controls of the reference set, and compares with baselines such as PRR and ROR | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 2–3 |
| Simulated evaluation of a dosing algorithm | On virtual patients generated from a population model fixed in advance, the concentration prediction error and the probability of target attainment of the algorithm are evaluated; the true parameters of the virtual patients are kept only in `tests/` | Pure computation |

## Sources of existing data

- Pharmacokinetics: the teaching datasets shipped with the R package `nlmixr2data` (theophylline, warfarin, phenobarbital and others); PK-DB (an open database curated from published clinical studies, with the features of the subjects, dosing regimens, pharmacokinetic parameters and concentration–time courses; browsing needs no account, downloading a dataset needs registration). Subject-level clinical pharmacology data is mostly not public, and the literature often gives mean curves only.
- Interactions: the tables published by FDA of example substrates, inhibitors and inducers of CYP enzymes and transporters; the model library of Open Systems Pharmacology and the clinical studies cited in its qualification reports; the Drug Interaction Database (DIDB) needs a subscription.
- Pharmacovigilance: the quarterly FAERS data files and the openFDA interface (public); VigiBase needs an application to the Uppsala Monitoring Centre, and research organizations and universities also pay a fee; reference sets include the OMOP reference set, the EU-ADR reference set and the time-indexed reference set, all with public labels.
- Combinations and labeling: the in vitro combination screening data of NCI-ALMANAC and DrugComb; the drug labels in DailyMed.
- Interaction ratios reported in the literature depend on dose, timing, route of administration and study population; check the study conditions one by one before using a ratio for comparison.

## Preventing answer leakage

- The AUC ratios of classic interactions were published long ago, and the open-source model library holds ready-made qualified models that yield the predictions once downloaded. Make the submitted model files the object of scoring, rerun by the agent judge at doses, timings or perpetrator drugs not listed in the task statement; or choose drug pairs the model library does not contain. The solving environment has no network access and does not contain the model library.
- The population parameter estimates of the teaching datasets appear in textbooks and software documentation. Hold out by subject, with the concentrations of the held-out subjects kept only in `tests/`; or simulate new subjects from a fixed model with a changed covariate distribution.
- The positive and negative labels of the reference sets are public, and a drug name alone leads by search to its known adverse reactions. Split by time: provide only the reports dated before the cutoff, and take adverse reactions added to the labeling afterwards as the positives; the task statement does not name the reference set.
- A signal detection task is scored on the ranking of statistics recomputed by the agent judge, not on the submitted ranking; and the solving trajectory is checked for known drug–event pairs written in directly.

## Tools and compute

- Population modeling: `nlmixr2` with `rxode2`, `saemix`, `mrgsolve` (simulation), Stan with Torsten, Pharmpy; Monolix and Phoenix NLME, like NONMEM, are commercial software.
- Model diagnostics and noncompartmental analysis: `xpose`, `vpc`, `npde`, `PKNCA`.
- Physiologically based pharmacokinetics: PK-Sim and MoBi of Open Systems Pharmacology (open source, GPLv2 license); Simcyp and GastroPlus are commercial software.
- Dose individualization: `mapbayr`, `posologyr` (maximum a posteriori Bayesian estimation).
- Pharmacovigilance and synergy: `openEBGM`, `pvda` (disproportionality metrics); `BIGL`, SynergyFinder (synergy); the MedDRA terminology hierarchy needs a license; RxNorm is free, and downloading its full release needs a free UMLS license.
- Compute: fitting a population model to a teaching-scale dataset takes minutes, and hours with hundreds of subjects and a covariate search; one population simulation of a physiologically based model takes minutes, and parameter calibration takes hours; the disproportionality computation over all quarterly FAERS files fits on a single machine. Rerunning the model and the metrics during review costs less than solving. These are rough estimates; the measurement in step 2 prevails.

## Common pitfalls

- The same set of clinical interaction data used both to calibrate the mechanistic parameters (Ki, the fraction metabolized fm) and to evaluate the predictions, which makes the agreement meaningless.
- Weak interactions judged by the twofold range. When the observed ratio is close to 1 the twofold range is passed almost inevitably; when Guest and colleagues switched to limits that vary with the ratio, prediction success fell by 21%, mostly at no or weak interaction (AUC ratio below 2).
- Gut-wall metabolism, route and timing of administration ignored. Oral midazolam undergoes first-pass metabolism by CYP3A in both the gut wall and the liver, and its AUC differs about 400-fold between coadministration with itraconazole and with rifampicin; the change after intravenous dosing is far smaller than after oral dosing.
- Mechanism-based inactivation (clarithromycin, for example) and induction both have a time course of onset and offset; computing them with the steady-state formula for competitive inhibition, or simulating too briefly for the enzyme level to reach steady state, biases the result. Four days after rifampicin was stopped, the AUC of oral midazolam was still only 13% of its value before treatment.
- In a population model, data below the limit of quantification simply deleted; collinear covariates (body weight and age) tested separately; empirical Bayes estimates of individual parameters still used to screen covariates or for diagnostics when shrinkage exceeds 20–30%.
- A model judged only by goodness-of-fit plots and the objective function value, with no simulation-based diagnostics and no evaluation on held-out subjects; objective function values compared across estimation algorithms or across datasets.
- A disproportionality metric taken for an incidence or a relative risk. Spontaneous reports have no number of exposed patients as a denominator, and they are affected by stimulated reporting (media coverage and regulatory communications), confounding by indication and masking.
- FAERS not deduplicated (one case has several versions), drug names not normalized (brand names and spelling variants), or concomitant drugs counted the same as suspect drugs.
- A signal stated as a confirmed adverse reaction; a model-predicted change in exposure stated directly as a dose adjustment recommendation.

## Example: physiologically based pharmacokinetic prediction of CYP3A4-mediated drug interactions

Midazolam is metabolized mainly by CYP3A and is a sensitive index substrate of CYP3A; in the FDA classification, a strong inhibitor raises the AUC of a sensitive index substrate 5-fold or more, and a strong inducer lowers the AUC of a sensitive substrate by 80% or more. In the crossover study of Olkkola and colleagues, ketoconazole or itraconazole raised the AUC of oral midazolam 10- to 15-fold; Backman and colleagues reported that pretreatment with rifampicin lowered it by 96%. A physiologically based pharmacokinetic model combines the physicochemical and in vitro parameters of a drug with human physiological parameters and is used to predict such interactions where no clinical study exists. Hanke and colleagues built an interaction network for CYP3A4 and P-gp on an open-source platform (the perpetrators rifampicin, itraconazole and clarithromycin, the victims midazolam, alfentanil and digoxin), using 112 studies for model building and 57 studies to test the predictions, and 93% of the predicted AUC ratios fell within twofold of the observed values. The task asks the solving agent to build the victim and perpetrator models independently and to predict the interaction ratios of held-out clinical studies.

The following is illustrated for the Prediction class; the class is assigned by the expert. Scientific discovery level 2, physical-world observation level 1.

- **Task statement and deliverables.** The task statement fixes the platform and version, the physiology database and the virtual population, and gives, for model building, the physicochemical properties of each drug, literature ranges for the in vitro metabolism, inhibition and induction parameters, concentration–time data after single-drug administration, and the data of some of the interaction studies; for the held-out interaction studies (with different doses, timings and routes) it gives the study design only, without the observed values. The deliverables are the model file of each drug, a `results.json` collecting the AUC ratio and Cmax ratio of each held-out study with their prediction intervals, and a short report. The format of the model files and of `results.json` is the unified output format.
- **Sub-questions.** Divided by scientific content, for illustration only:
  1. Disposition of the victim drug: the fractions of midazolam metabolized by CYP3A4 in the gut wall and in the liver, and the concentration–time curves after oral and intravenous dosing.
  2. Inhibition: the effect of itraconazole (competitive inhibition) and of clarithromycin (mechanism-based inactivation) on midazolam exposure.
  3. Induction: the induction of CYP3A4 by rifampicin, with the time course of its onset and offset.
- **Given and deliverable.** The forms shown are for illustration; the expert decides which each sub-question needs. In every sub-question the given is the target quantity as defined in the task statement; the true value is not provided to the solving agent. The deliverable is the prediction with its uncertainty, as text (structure and approximations: whether gut-wall metabolism and enzyme turnover are included, checked against the single-drug data) and as computation (the simulated ratios with their prediction intervals, and a sensitivity analysis on key parameters such as fm, Ki or kinact, and the enzyme turnover rate). The comparison with the observed values of the held-out studies is made by the agent judge.
- **Checks.** The agent judge reruns the submitted models on the fixed platform and checks the ratios the submitter reports; the AUC and Cmax of the single-drug curves must be within twofold of the observed values before the interactions are scored; the AUC ratios of the held-out studies are judged by the Guest criterion, giving the fraction within the limits and the geometric mean fold error, and the observed values are checked against the stated prediction intervals. The qualification report of the platform itself obtains a geometric mean fold error of 1.4 on 135 clinical observations, which can serve as a reference for the tolerance; the tolerances are provisional and are set from the performance of the reference model in step 2.
- **Calibration cases.** The empty submission (score 0); literature ratios copied without model files, or models whose rerun disagrees with the reported values (a shortcut; the corresponding values score 0); a result estimated from a static formula without gut-wall metabolism and time course (a low-accuracy method); a submission with correct values but no prediction intervals; a submission that completes only the single-drug models and inhibition, without induction (partially correct); the reference model (a known result).
- **Other tasks of the same structure.** Change the enzyme or transporter (CYP2D6, CYP2C8, P-gp), or the population (children, patients with hepatic or renal impairment); the checks stay the same, and the comparison data becomes the corresponding clinical studies.
