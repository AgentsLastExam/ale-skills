# Medicine: health informatics and clinical AI

This file adds content specific to health informatics and clinical AI to `references/disciplines/medicine.md`, whose provisions still apply.

Scope: modeling and phenotyping of electronic health record data (computable phenotypes and their validation, missingness and informed presence bias, common data models and federated queries), clinical prediction models (development and calibration of risk models, temporal validation and dataset shift, subgroup performance and algorithmic bias), decision support and deployment (alerting, silent trials and alert fatigue, clinical language models and documentation), standards and interoperability (exchange standards, terminology mapping, clinical ontologies and coding), and privacy and distributed learning (de-identification and re-identification risk, federated learning). For monitoring algorithms that take waveforms and continuous vital signs as input see `physiological-signals.md`; for imaging models see `medical-imaging.md`; for causal questions that estimate treatment effects from electronic health records see `epidemiology.md`; for evaluating the effect of a deployed model on care processes and patient outcomes see `health-systems.md`.

## Screening criteria

- Build with priority: risk-model questions in which the data is released under an open license, the outcome and the time of prediction are clearly defined, and a ready baseline exists to compare against. An example is predicting in-hospital death from the records of the first 48 hours after ICU admission: the 12,000 records of the PhysioNet/CinC Challenge 2012 can be downloaded publicly, SAPS-I serves as the baseline, and both discrimination and calibration have metrics defined in advance.
- Build with priority: external validation of a model or rule that is already fixed, where the conclusion is a set of values for discrimination, calibration and alert burden. An example is the external validation of a widely deployed proprietary sepsis alert model: across 38,455 hospitalizations the AUROC was 0.63, and at the threshold in use 18% of hospitalized patients triggered an alert while 67% of sepsis cases were still missed. When the data may be put into a task, the check for such a task is simply the recomputation of the metrics.
- Build with priority: algorithm-audit questions in which the conclusion is a choice between mechanisms that can be tested by ablation, by replacing the label and retraining. An example is the racial bias of an algorithm used to manage high-need patients: at the same risk score Black patients were sicker, because the algorithm used health care cost instead of illness as its prediction label; the authors released synthetic data with properties close to the original.
- Defer: tasks whose only data source is controlled-access data such as MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD or the n2c2 corpora, for example temporal validation across the year groups of MIMIC-IV, or evaluating a de-identification method on the 2014 i2b2/UTHealth corpus. Such data cannot be put into a task package, and the solving agent has no way to obtain it. Discuss with the expert a switch to data under an open license (demo subsets, challenge data, synthetic data) or to the expert's own data that may be put into a task; the structure of the question stays the same.
- Defer: tasks judged by clinical benefit, alert fatigue or clinician uptake after a model goes live, for example whether an alerting system lowers mortality. Such conclusions need a prospective silent trial or a clinical trial and cannot be observed within six hours. The task can instead examine, on retrospective data, the alert burden and lead time of an alerting policy fixed in advance.

## Matters to confirm in conversation

- The time of prediction, the observation window and the prediction window: when the model makes its prediction, which data before that time it may use, and within what period the outcome must occur.
- The operational definition of the outcome: based on diagnosis codes, laboratory results, orders or manual chart review; whether the definition depends on clinical actions (for example sepsis defined through antibiotic orders and cultures).
- The kind of validation and the question it answers: internal validation, temporal validation or external validation across institutions; how development and validation data differ in period, institution, coding system and case mix. The field uses TRIPOD+AI as the reporting standard for prediction models and PROBAST+AI to assess risk of bias and applicability.
- The intended use of the model and its decision threshold: screening, triage or alerting; the acceptable alert burden (alerts per true positive); whether net benefit must be reported.
- The subgroups whose performance must be reported separately (age, sex, race, department), and how well the subgroup variables are recorded.
- What missingness means: whether the absence of a test itself carries information, whether the model is allowed to use it, and whether that information is stable in the deployment setting.
- The source and quality of the reference standard: the annotation guideline for chart review, the number of annotators and their agreement, and how the reviewed sample was drawn (whether only algorithm-positive cases were reviewed).
- The access level of the data, and whether the way the agent reads the data complies with the data use agreement: controlled-access data on PhysioNet must not be handed to third-party online services, and its official guidance also suggests how such services may be used within the agreement.

## Checking templates

The templates below add to the general templates in `references/disciplines/medicine.md`.

| Template | Criterion | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Metrics recomputed from per-case probabilities | With the held-out labels, the agent judge recomputes AUROC, AUPRC, Brier score, calibration intercept and calibration slope, with bootstrap confidence intervals, from the submitted per-case predicted probabilities; the values the submitter reports are not used | Uses existing data, must pass |
| Decay under temporal or external validation | Discrimination and calibration are recomputed on data outside the development period or institution and compared with the expected performance and interval the submitter declared in advance; a true value outside the declared interval counts as overestimation | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 2–3 |
| Subgroup performance | Discrimination, calibration and sensitivity at a fixed threshold are computed separately for subgroups specified in advance, with subgroup sizes and confidence intervals; the gaps between subgroups agree with what the submitter declared | Uses existing data, counts for part of the score |
| Alert burden and net benefit | At the threshold or alert budget fixed in the task statement, sensitivity, positive predictive value, alerts per true positive and the net benefit of the decision curve are recomputed | Uses existing data |
| Validation of a computable phenotype | The agent judge executes the submitted phenotype definition again on held-out chart-review labels and computes positive predictive value, sensitivity and F1; the sampling weights of the reviewed sample enter the estimates | Uses existing data, scientific discovery level 1–2 |
| Entity-level scoring of de-identification | Compared with held-out manual annotations, entity-level recall and precision are computed for each category of protected health information, with recall given priority; every missed direct identifier is listed | Uses existing data |
| Audit of feature timing | The agent judge reruns the submitted feature-building code and confirms that every feature has a timestamp earlier than the time of prediction; with the data after the time of prediction cut off, the predictions must stay unchanged | Must pass |

## Sources of existing data

- Critical-care data under open licenses: the PhysioNet/CinC Challenge 2012 (12,000 ICU records drawn from MIMIC-II); the MIMIC-IV demo subset (100 patients) and the eICU-CRD demo subset (over 2,500 unit stays from 20 hospitals). The demo subsets are very small; they suit a check of the workflow and cannot support conclusions on calibration or subgroups.
- Controlled-access data: MIMIC-IV (patients admitted in 2008–2022; dates are shifted per patient and only three-year year groups are given, which allow a split by time) and MIMIC-IV-Note (discharge summaries and radiology reports). Data derived from them and models trained on them are likewise treated as containing sensitive information and cannot be put into a task package.
- Clinical text corpora: the corpora of the successive n2c2 (formerly i2b2) shared tasks, for example the 2014 i2b2/UTHealth de-identification corpus (1,304 records of 296 patients, with protected information replaced by realistic surrogates), for which each user must register and sign a data use agreement; ACI-Bench (207 doctor–patient dialogues with their clinical notes) is released under CC BY 4.0.
- Phenotype definitions and terminologies: PheKB holds phenotype algorithms validated by chart review, with the algorithms and validation results only and no patient data; the OHDSI standardized vocabularies are downloaded through Athena, and some of them need a separate license; SNOMED CT and UMLS can be used only under license.
- Synthetic data: synthetic patient records generated by Synthea (output as FHIR and CSV), suited to tasks on interoperability, terminology mapping and query correctness; predictive performance obtained on synthetic data does not represent performance on real data.

## Preventing answer leakage

- The labels of public challenges can now all be downloaded (the outcome files of all three sets of the PhysioNet/CinC Challenge 2012 are public). The solving environment has no network access; held-out records are renumbered and shuffled, with record identifiers that match the public files removed; the labels are kept only in `tests/`.
- Information produced after the time of prediction mixed into the features is the most common leak in this subdomain: discharge diagnosis codes, discharge disposition, length of stay, documentation entered afterwards. The task statement states the time of prediction, and the agent judge runs the audit of feature timing.
- Action variables on which the outcome definition depends leak the label. When the outcome is defined through antibiotic orders and cultures, those orders and features derived from them cannot be inputs.
- Coefficients of published models, AUROC values and leaderboard scores can be found by search. Make the submitted per-case probabilities and the rerunnable code the object of scoring; the task statement contains no challenge name, paper title or dataset entry ID.
- In clinical text tasks, all records of one patient go to the same side of the split; a model that learns the rule by which the realistic surrogates were generated shows inflated recall, so the held-out corpus should come from another source or use another surrogate scheme.

## Tools and compute

- Model building and validation: scikit-learn, statsmodels; in R, rms (calibration curves and internal validation), pmsampsize (sample size) and dcurves (decision curves).
- Electronic health records and standards: PatientLevelPrediction (model building and external validation on OMOP data) and PheValuator (evaluation of phenotype algorithms) from OHDSI; YAIB (a unified pipeline for critical-care prediction tasks, shipped with demo data only); HAPI FHIR.
- Clinical text and de-identification: spaCy and medspaCy; Philter, Microsoft Presidio.
- Federated learning and fairness: Flower, NVIDIA FLARE; FLamby provides loaders and benchmark code for medical datasets naturally partitioned by institution, and users must download the data themselves under each dataset's license; Fairlearn.
- Compute: a risk model on tabular data trains in minutes to hours on CPUs, and the recomputation of metrics with bootstrap during review takes minutes; fine-tuning and inference of clinical language models need GPUs. These are rough estimates; the measurement in step 2 prevails.

## Common pitfalls

- The time of prediction not stated, or a feature window that extends past it. Data from the whole hospital stay used to "predict" an outcome during the stay gives inflated metrics and a model that cannot be used clinically.
- Performance reported only on a randomly split internal test set and stated as performance after deployment. A change of coding system (such as the switch from ICD-9 to ICD-10), of laboratory methods or of case mix makes a model drift over time; temporal and external validation answer a different question from internal validation.
- No recalibration after training with oversampling or class weights, so the output probabilities are systematically too high; or calibration supported only by the P value of the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with no calibration intercept, calibration slope or calibration curve.
- Whether and when a test was ordered treated as an ordinary feature. In one study, for 233 of 272 laboratory tests the presence of an order was itself associated with three-year survival; such information reflects clinicians' judgement and workflow and may not hold at another institution.
- When the outcome label is defined by clinical actions, the model learns that clinicians already suspect the condition, not the disease itself, and the lead time of the alert is overestimated.
- Subgroups too small, with confidence intervals so wide that a gap cannot be told from noise, yet a conclusion of fairness or unfairness drawn from them; or subgroup variables that are themselves missing or recorded inconsistently.
- Bias introduced by the choice of prediction label: a convenient quantity such as cost or number of visits used in place of health need can perform well on some accuracy metrics and still produce large disparities between groups.
- A phenotype algorithm for which only algorithm-positive cases were reviewed, which yields positive predictive value alone, reported with a sensitivity; or positive predictive values compared directly between institutions with different prevalence.
- De-identification judged by overall F1 alone. Missing a name and missing a year have different consequences, so recall must be reported by category; agreement between annotators and the gold standard is itself limited (in the 2014 corpus the annotators' mean F1 against the gold standard was 0.927), and the tolerance cannot be tighter than that.

## Example: predicting in-hospital death from the first 48 hours of ICU records

ICU severity scores are among the earliest applications of clinical prediction models: scores such as SAPS and APACHE estimate the risk of in-hospital death from physiological measurements early after admission and are used for case-mix adjustment and quality comparison, so both discrimination and calibration are required. The PhysioNet/CinC Challenge 2012 turned this question into a public evaluation: 12,000 adult ICU records drawn from MIMIC-II, with ICU stays shorter than 48 hours excluded, divided equally into three sets, and an in-hospital mortality of 14.2%; each record gives admission descriptors and irregularly sampled time series of more than thirty vital signs and laboratory values over the first 48 hours, and is marked with one of four ICU types (coronary care, cardiac surgery recovery, medical, surgical). The evaluation had two metrics: the smaller of sensitivity and positive predictive value, and a range-normalized Hosmer–Lemeshow statistic computed over deciles of risk. The SAPS-I baseline scored 0.3125 and 68.58 on the two, and the best results of that year were 0.5353 and 17.88. The data is released under an open license and may be put into a task package; it carries no calendar time and cannot be split by time, so this example replaces temporal validation with transfer between ICU types.

The following is illustrated for the Prediction class; the class is assigned by the expert. Scientific discovery level 2, physical-world observation level 1.

- **Task statement and deliverables.** The task statement gives 8,000 development records with outcomes and 4,000 held-out records (without outcomes, renumbered), states that the time of prediction is 48 hours after admission, and names one ICU type whose records cannot be used for development in sub-question 2; it provides neither the name of the challenge nor published scores. The deliverables are per-case probabilities of death for the held-out records in `predictions.csv` (one column each for the full model, the transfer model of sub-question 2 and the ablated model of sub-question 3), the expected performance declared in advance with its intervals in `results.json`, rerunnable code and a short report. The format of `predictions.csv` and `results.json` is the unified output format.
- **Sub-questions.** Divided by scientific content, for illustration only:
  1. Overall risk: give the probability of in-hospital death for the held-out records, with discrimination and calibration both holding.
  2. Transfer across case mix: develop a model on the records of the other three ICU types only, predict for the patients of the excluded type, and give the change in calibration after transfer and the effect of recalibration.
  3. Informed presence: separate how much of the performance comes from the measured values themselves and how much from "which items were measured and how often", and state whether the latter still holds when the care process changes.
- **Given and deliverable.** The forms shown are for illustration; the expert decides which each sub-question needs. In every sub-question the given is the target quantity as defined in the task statement; the true value is not provided to the solving agent. The deliverable is the prediction with its uncertainty, as text (the model form, the variables used, the handling of missing values, and an argument that it applies to the target population) and as computation (per-case probabilities, with sensitivity analysis on the handling of missing values, the choice of variables and recalibration; in sub-question 3 the predictions made again without measurement-frequency and missingness-indicator features are the ablation of the Prediction class; the AUROC, calibration intercept and calibration slope declared in advance, with their intervals). The comparison with the held-out outcomes is made by the agent judge.
- **Checks.** The agent judge reruns the code, confirms that it reproduces the submitted probabilities, and runs the audit of feature timing; from the probabilities and the held-out outcomes it then recomputes AUROC, Brier score, calibration intercept and calibration slope, the two metrics of the original evaluation, and the corresponding values within each ICU type, age band and sex. The criteria are: overall performance better than the SAPS-I baseline; a confidence interval of the calibration slope that contains 1; every true value inside the interval the submitter declared. The tolerance is the bootstrap resampling spread on the held-out set, set from trial runs.
- **Calibration cases.** The empty submission (score 0); the same mortality output for every record (a shortcut, correct calibration overall and no discrimination at all); submitted probabilities that match the held-out outcomes almost perfectly while the code cannot reproduce them (a shortcut, taken from the public outcome files, score 0); a model trained with class weights and not recalibrated (good discrimination, probabilities systematically too high, partially correct); SAPS-I (the baseline).
- **Other tasks of the same structure.** When the expert holds data with calendar time that may be put into a task, replace sub-question 2 with temporal validation: develop on an earlier period and check the decay of performance and the effect of recalibration on a later one; or change the object to outcomes such as emergency triage or readmission. The workflow and the checks stay the same.
